## Lesson 1: What to Do First

# Welcome to Your California Homebuying Journey!

Congratulations on taking the first step toward homeownership! Whether you've been dreaming about this for years or just started thinking seriously about buying, you're in the right place. This course is designed specifically for California homebuyers like you, and we're going to walk through everything you need to know—step by step.

Here's the truth: buying a home in California can feel overwhelming. The prices are high, the competition can be fierce, and there's a lot of paperwork involved. But here's the good news—thousands of Californians just like you buy homes every single month. With the right preparation and knowledge, you can absolutely do this.

## Your First Week: The Foundation

Before we dive into mortgages, down payment programs, and house hunting, we need to build your foundation. Think of this like preparing for a marathon—you wouldn't just show up on race day without training, right?

### Step 1: Know Your Credit Score (Day 1-2)

Your credit score is one of the most important numbers in the homebuying process. It affects:
- Whether you qualify for a mortgage
- What interest rate you'll receive
- Which loan programs are available to you
- How much home you can afford

**Here's what to do:**
Pull your free credit reports from all three bureaus at **AnnualCreditReport.com** (this is the official site—avoid imposters). You're entitled to one free report from each bureau every year.

Look for:
- Your credit scores from each bureau (they may differ slightly)
- Any errors or accounts you don't recognize
- Late payments or collections that need addressing
- How much of your available credit you're using

**What the numbers mean for California homebuyers:**
- **740+**: Excellent. You'll qualify for the best rates and all loan programs
- **700-739**: Very good. Most programs available, competitive rates
- **660-699**: Good. FHA and many conventional options available
- **620-659**: Fair. FHA loans available, some conventional options
- **580-619**: Limited options, but FHA may still work
- **Below 580**: You'll likely need to work on credit improvement first

Don't panic if your score isn't perfect. There are loan programs for various credit situations, and we'll cover strategies to improve your score if needed.

### Step 2: Get Honest About Your Finances (Day 2-3)

Grab a notebook or open a spreadsheet. We're going to get real about where you stand financially.

**Calculate your monthly income:**
- Gross income (before taxes) from all sources
- Include base salary, bonuses (if consistent), overtime, commissions
- If self-employed, you'll use your tax returns—we'll cover this in detail later

**List your monthly debts:**
- Car payments
- Student loans
- Credit card minimum payments
- Personal loans
- Child support or alimony
- Any other recurring debt obligations

**Assess your savings:**
- Checking and savings accounts
- Investment accounts
- Retirement accounts (yes, some can be used for home purchase)
- Gift funds available from family
- Any other assets

**Why this matters:** Lenders will look at your debt-to-income ratio (DTI). In California, most lenders want your total monthly debts (including your future mortgage) to be below 43-50% of your gross monthly income. Knowing your numbers now helps you understand what's realistic.

### Step 3: Start Your Down Payment Strategy (Day 3-4)

Here's something many first-time buyers don't realize: **you don't need 20% down to buy a home in California.**

In fact, there are programs that allow:
- **3% down** (Conventional loans)
- **3.5% down** (FHA loans)
- **0% down** (VA loans for veterans, USDA for rural areas)
- **Down payment assistance** that can cover some or all of your down payment

We'll dive deep into California's down payment assistance programs later in this course, including CalHFA MyHome, CalHFA ZIP, GSFA, and the City of Los Angeles MIPA program. These programs have helped thousands of California buyers who thought homeownership was out of reach.

For now, know this: keep saving, but don't assume you need a massive down payment to get started.

### Step 4: Start Dreaming (But Realistically) (Day 5-7)

Now for the fun part! Start thinking about where you want to live and what you need in a home.

**Consider:**
- What cities or neighborhoods interest you?
- How long is your maximum acceptable commute?
- How many bedrooms and bathrooms do you need?
- Do you need a yard? A garage? A home office?
- Are good schools a priority?
- What's your timeline—when do you want to be in your new home?

**Pro tip:** Start casually browsing listings online (Zillow, Redfin, Realtor.com) to get a sense of what's available in your price range and target areas. This isn't serious house hunting yet—it's market research.

## Common First-Timer Mistakes to Avoid

❌ **Making big purchases before buying** - Don't buy a new car, open new credit cards, or make major purchases. This can hurt your qualification.

❌ **Changing jobs** - Lenders like stability. If possible, avoid job changes until after you close.

❌ **Moving money around** - Large, unexplained deposits in your bank accounts create problems. Keep your finances stable and documentable.

❌ **Waiting until you're "ready"** - Many buyers wait too long thinking they need to be in a perfect position. Talk to a lender early—you might be closer than you think.

## Lesson 2: Your Homebuying Timeline

# Mapping Your Path to Homeownership

One of the biggest questions first-time buyers ask is: "How long does this whole process take?" The answer depends on where you're starting from—but here's the complete picture so you can plan accordingly.

## The Two Phases of Homebuying

Think of homebuying as having two distinct phases:

**Phase 1: Preparation (1-6 months before you start looking)**
This is where you get your finances in order, understand your buying power, and get pre-approved. If you're already in good shape, this might take just a few weeks. If you need to improve credit or save more, it could take several months.

**Phase 2: The Transaction (30-60 days)**
Once you find a home and get an offer accepted, closing typically takes 30-45 days in California, sometimes up to 60 days for certain loan programs.

Let's break down each phase in detail.

## Phase 1: Getting Ready

### If Your Credit and Finances Are Strong (2-4 weeks prep)

You might be ready to move quickly if you:
- Have a credit score of 620 or higher
- Have stable income that's documentable
- Have enough savings for down payment and closing costs (or qualify for assistance)
- Have been at your job for 2+ years (or in the same field)

**Your timeline:**
- Week 1: Gather documents, research loan options
- Week 2: Apply for pre-approval
- Week 3-4: Receive pre-approval, start house hunting

### If You Need Some Preparation (2-6 months)

You might need more time if you:
- Need to improve your credit score
- Need to save more for down payment or closing costs
- Recently changed jobs or are newly self-employed
- Have some financial issues to clean up

**Your timeline:**
- Months 1-3: Work on credit improvement, save aggressively
- Months 3-5: Continue saving, start soft shopping for homes
- Month 5-6: Apply for pre-approval when ready

### Credit Improvement Timeline

If your score needs work, here's what's realistic:
- **Paying down credit cards (1-2 months):** Reducing your credit utilization can boost your score within one billing cycle
- **Disputing errors (1-2 months):** The bureaus have 30 days to investigate
- **Building payment history (3-6 months):** Showing consistent on-time payments takes time
- **Waiting for negative items to age (6-24 months):** Some issues just need time

## Phase 2: The Transaction Timeline

Once you're pre-approved and start seriously looking, here's what the transaction looks like:

### House Hunting (Varies: 2 weeks to 6 months)

This is the wildcard in your timeline. In a hot California market, you might make multiple offers before one gets accepted. In a slower market, you might find your home quickly.

**Factors that affect how long you'll search:**
- How specific your requirements are
- Your price range relative to the market
- How competitive your offer can be
- The current market conditions in your target area

### Under Contract to Closing (30-45 days typical)

Once your offer is accepted, the clock starts. Here's the standard California timeline:

**Days 1-3: Open Escrow**
- Sign initial contract documents
- Submit earnest money deposit (typically 1-3% of purchase price)
- Escrow company opens your file

**Days 1-10: Inspections**
- Schedule and complete home inspection
- Order any specialized inspections (termite, roof, sewer, etc.)
- Review inspection reports
- Negotiate repairs or credits if needed

**Days 7-17: Contingency Period**
- Complete your investigation of the property
- Review HOA documents (if applicable)
- Make your decision to proceed or negotiate

**Days 14-21: Appraisal**
- Lender orders appraisal
- Appraiser visits the property
- Appraisal report delivered
- Address any appraisal issues if they arise

**Days 21-35: Loan Processing and Underwriting**
- Submit all requested documents
- Underwriter reviews your complete file
- Respond to any conditions or requests
- Receive final loan approval ("clear to close")

**Days 35-40: Closing Preparation**
- Receive Closing Disclosure (at least 3 business days before closing)
- Review all final numbers
- Complete final walkthrough of the property
- Wire funds for closing

**Closing Day (Day 40-45):**
- Sign final documents
- Lender funds the loan
- Deed records with the county
- Receive your keys!

## California-Specific Timeline Considerations

### Escrow Periods

In California, escrow is handled by a neutral third-party escrow company (not attorneys like in some other states). This generally makes the process efficient, but you need to stay responsive to requests.

### Contingency Periods

California's standard purchase agreement (the CAR forms) has specific contingency periods:
- **Inspection contingency:** Typically 17 days
- **Appraisal contingency:** Typically 17 days
- **Loan contingency:** Typically 21 days

These can be negotiated shorter in competitive situations, but be careful about removing protections too early.

### Competitive Markets

In hot California markets (parts of the Bay Area, LA, San Diego, etc.), you may need to:
- Be ready to tour homes immediately when they hit the market
- Submit offers within days of a home being listed
- Compete against multiple offers
- Consider shorter contingency periods

## Building Your Team

As you prepare, you'll want to assemble your homebuying team:

**Loan Officer (That's us!)**
Start here. Your loan officer will help you understand your buying power and get pre-approved. Do this BEFORE you start seriously house hunting.

**Real Estate Agent**
Find an agent who knows your target area well. They should be responsive, knowledgeable, and someone you feel comfortable with. Ask for recommendations from friends and family.

**Escrow Officer**
Usually selected when you open escrow, but your agent may have recommendations.

**Home Inspector**
Your agent will likely have recommendations, or you can research inspectors in your area. Look for experience and thorough reports.

## Lesson 3: Homebuyer Readiness Assessment

# Are You Ready to Buy? Let's Find Out

Before you dive headfirst into the homebuying process, let's do a thorough self-assessment. This isn't meant to discourage you—it's meant to help you understand exactly where you stand and what steps you might need to take.

Be honest with yourself as you go through this assessment. If you're not quite ready, that's okay! Knowing what you need to work on is the first step toward getting there.

## Financial Readiness Check

### Your Credit Profile

**Check your credit scores and answer honestly:**

| Your Score Range | What It Means |
|-----------------|---------------|
| 740+ | You're in excellent shape. You'll have access to the best rates and all loan programs. |
| 700-739 | Very good! You'll qualify for most programs with competitive rates. |
| 660-699 | Good. You have solid options including FHA and many conventional programs. |
| 620-659 | Fair. FHA is definitely an option, and some conventional programs may work. |
| 580-619 | Limited but possible. FHA loans may be available with 10% down. |
| Below 580 | Consider focusing on credit improvement before applying. |

**Credit red flags to address:**
- [ ] Any accounts in collections?
- [ ] Any late payments in the past 12 months?
- [ ] Any bankruptcies or foreclosures in the past 2-7 years?
- [ ] Credit utilization above 30%?
- [ ] Errors on your credit report?

If you checked any boxes, don't panic—but do address these issues. Many can be resolved, and we can discuss your specific situation.

### Your Income Stability

Lenders want to see stable, documentable income. Here's what they're looking for:

**For W-2 Employees:**
- ✅ 2 years of continuous employment history
- ✅ Consistent or increasing income
- ✅ Same employer preferred (but same industry is often fine)

**For Self-Employed Borrowers:**
- ✅ 2 years of tax returns showing the business
- ✅ Stable or increasing income over those 2 years
- ✅ Documented business ownership (license, CPA letter, etc.)

**Income situations that need extra attention:**
- Commission or bonus income (typically needs 2-year history)
- Part-time or gig economy income
- Less than 2 years on the job
- Recent career change
- Gaps in employment

Don't disqualify yourself if you're in one of these situations—talk to a loan officer. There may be solutions you're not aware of.

### Your Debt-to-Income Ratio

Your DTI is crucial. Here's how to calculate it:

**Step 1:** Add up your monthly debt payments
- Car payment(s): $______
- Student loan payment(s): $______
- Credit card minimum payments: $______
- Personal loans: $______
- Child support/alimony: $______
- Other monthly debt: $______
- **Total monthly debts:** $______

**Step 2:** Calculate your gross monthly income (before taxes)
- **Gross monthly income:** $______

**Step 3:** Divide debts by income
- Total debts ÷ Gross income = ______%

**What this means:**
- **Under 36%:** Excellent. You have room for a healthy mortgage payment.
- **36-43%:** Good. You're in a solid range for most loan programs.
- **43-50%:** Moderate. Some programs will work; others won't. We can help you strategize.
- **Over 50%:** High. You may need to pay down debt before qualifying, or you may need a specific loan program.

**Keep in mind:** This calculation is BEFORE adding your future mortgage payment. Lenders typically want your total DTI (including your new mortgage) to stay under 43-50%.

### Your Savings and Assets

What do you have available for your home purchase?

**For down payment and closing costs:**
- Savings accounts: $______
- Checking accounts: $______
- Investment accounts: $______
- Gift funds available: $______
- Other liquid assets: $______
- **Total available:** $______

**Estimated costs in California:**
- Down payment: 3-20% of purchase price (but assistance may help!)
- Closing costs: Typically 2-4% of purchase price
- Reserves: Some programs want 2+ months of payments in the bank after closing

**Example:** For a $500,000 home purchase:
- Minimum down (3%): $15,000
- Closing costs (3%): $15,000
- Moving/initial costs: $5,000
- **Minimum needed:** $35,000 (without down payment assistance)
- **With assistance:** Could be as low as $15,000-20,000

We'll cover down payment assistance programs in detail in Module 4!

- [ ] HOA fees and rules (if applicable)
- [ ] Unexpected costs (something always breaks)

**Reality check:** Experts recommend budgeting 1-3% of your home's value annually for maintenance. For a $500,000 home, that's $5,000-15,000 per year.

## Lesson 1: Understanding PITI and Your Monthly Payment

# What Will Your Mortgage Payment Really Be?

Here's something that surprises many first-time buyers: your mortgage payment isn't just the loan amount divided by the number of months. There are several components that make up your monthly housing payment, and understanding all of them is crucial for knowing what you can actually afford.

Let's break it down.

## The Four Parts of PITI

PITI stands for **Principal, Interest, Taxes, and Insurance**—the four main components of your monthly mortgage payment.

### Principal

This is the portion of your payment that actually pays down your loan balance. It's the money that builds your equity—the ownership stake you have in your home.

**Here's something interesting:** In the early years of your mortgage, very little of your payment goes toward principal. Most goes to interest. But as time goes on, more and more of your payment chips away at the principal. This is called amortization.

**Example:** On a $450,000 loan at 7% interest:
- Month 1: About $637 goes to principal, $2,625 goes to interest
- Year 10: About $1,028 goes to principal, $2,234 goes to interest
- Year 25: About $2,306 goes to principal, $956 goes to interest

This is why making extra payments toward principal—especially early on—can save you tens of thousands of dollars over the life of your loan.

### Interest

Interest is the cost of borrowing money. It's how lenders make money on your loan. Your interest rate has a massive impact on both your monthly payment and the total cost of your home.

**How much does the rate matter? Let's look at a $450,000 loan over 30 years:**

| Interest Rate | Monthly P&I | Total Interest Paid |
|--------------|-------------|---------------------|
| 6.0% | $2,698 | $521,460 |
| 6.5% | $2,844 | $573,999 |
| 7.0% | $2,995 | $628,163 |
| 7.5% | $3,147 | $683,931 |

That's over $150,000 difference in total interest between 6% and 7.5%! This is why getting the best rate you can qualify for matters so much.

### Taxes (Property Taxes)

In California, property taxes are based on your home's assessed value. Thanks to Proposition 13, your property tax assessment is set when you buy (at the purchase price) and can only increase by a maximum of 2% per year after that.

**California's base property tax rate is 1% of assessed value.** However, local add-ons for schools, bonds, and special districts often bring the effective rate to 1.1-1.4% in many areas.

**Example:** For a $500,000 home at 1.2% effective rate:
- Annual property taxes: $6,000
- Monthly escrow for taxes: $500

**Watch out for:** Mello-Roos taxes! These are special taxes in certain California developments (often newer communities) that fund local infrastructure. They can add $2,000-$15,000+ per year to your tax bill. Always check before making an offer!

### Insurance (Homeowners Insurance)

Your lender will require you to have homeowners insurance to protect their investment (and yours). In California, insurance costs have been rising significantly, especially in wildfire-prone areas.

**Typical California homeowners insurance costs:**
- Low-risk areas: $1,000-$2,000/year
- Moderate-risk areas: $2,000-$4,000/year
- High-risk fire areas: $4,000-$10,000+/year

**California's unique insurance challenges:**
- Many insurers have stopped writing new policies in fire zones
- You may need the California FAIR Plan as a last resort (basic coverage)
- Always get insurance quotes EARLY in your home search for properties in fire or flood zones

## Beyond PITI: The Other Costs

Your full monthly housing payment often includes more than just PITI:

### Private Mortgage Insurance (PMI)

If your down payment is less than 20%, you'll likely pay mortgage insurance. This protects the lender if you default.

**Approximate PMI costs:**
- Conventional loans: 0.5-1.5% of loan amount per year
- FHA loans: 0.55% of loan amount per year (for most borrowers)

**Example:** On a $450,000 loan with PMI at 0.8%:
- Annual PMI: $3,600
- Monthly PMI: $300

**Good news for conventional loans:** PMI can be removed once you reach 20% equity. For FHA loans, you'll have mortgage insurance for the life of the loan (unless you refinance to a conventional loan later).

### HOA Fees

If you're buying a condo, townhouse, or home in a planned community, you'll likely have Homeowners Association (HOA) fees.

**California HOA fees vary wildly:**
- Basic HOA (exterior maintenance only): $100-$300/month
- Condos with amenities: $300-$600/month
- Luxury condos or high-rise buildings: $600-$1,500+/month

**What HOA fees typically cover:**
- Common area maintenance
- Exterior building maintenance (for condos)
- Community amenities (pool, gym, etc.)
- Some utilities (varies)
- Reserve fund for major repairs

**Important:** When calculating affordability, you MUST include HOA fees. A home with a $500/month HOA has the equivalent of $500/month added to your payment.

## Putting It All Together: A Real Payment Example

Let's look at what a California home purchase might actually cost monthly:

**Scenario: $550,000 home purchase with 5% down**

| Component | Monthly Amount |
|-----------|----------------|
| Principal & Interest (7% rate on $522,500 loan) | $3,476 |
| Property Taxes (1.2% annually) | $550 |
| Homeowners Insurance | $200 |
| PMI (0.7% annually) | $305 |
| HOA (if applicable) | $350 |
| **Total Monthly Payment** | **$4,881** |

Seeing this full picture helps you understand what you're really signing up for—and helps you search for homes in the right price range.

## The 28/36 Rule of Thumb

Lenders use guidelines called "debt-to-income ratios" to determine what you can afford:

**Front-end ratio (housing costs):** Your total housing payment should be no more than 28-31% of your gross monthly income.

**Back-end ratio (total debts):** Your total monthly debts (housing + car + student loans + credit cards + other) should be no more than 43-50% of your gross monthly income.

**Example:**
- Gross monthly income: $12,000
- 28% of income: $3,360 (max recommended housing payment)
- 43% of income: $5,160 (max recommended total debt)

If you have a $400 car payment and $200 in student loans, that leaves $4,560 for housing under the 43% guideline—but ideally, you'd keep housing around $3,360 or less.

## Lesson 2: Comfort Budget vs Maximum Approval

# Don't Max Out Your Mortgage

Here's a secret that every homebuyer needs to hear: **just because a lender says you CAN borrow $600,000 doesn't mean you SHOULD.**

Your maximum approval amount is based on formulas and ratios. Your comfort budget is based on your actual life—and those are often two very different numbers.

## How Lenders Calculate Your Maximum

Lenders look at:
- Your gross monthly income (before taxes)
- Your monthly debt payments
- Your credit score
- Current interest rates
- The loan program you're using

They plug these numbers into their guidelines and determine the maximum loan amount you qualify for. But here's what they DON'T consider:

- Your retirement contributions
- Your kids' college savings
- Your vacation budget
- Your hobbies and entertainment
- Your emergency fund needs
- Childcare costs (unless it's court-ordered)
- Your dining out and lifestyle preferences
- Your other savings goals
- Variable expenses that don't show on credit reports

See the problem? The formula looks at your debts, not your life.

## The Max-Out Trap

We've seen it happen: buyers qualify for a high amount, get excited, buy at the top of their budget, and then feel "house poor." Every dollar goes to the mortgage, and there's nothing left for anything else.

**Signs you might be stretching too far:**
- You'd have to cut your retirement contributions
- You'd have minimal savings left each month
- You'd have to give up activities you enjoy
- You'd have no buffer for unexpected expenses
- You'd stress about money regularly

That's no way to enjoy your new home.

## Finding Your Comfort Number

Instead of asking, "What's the maximum I can borrow?" ask, "What payment lets me live the life I want?"

### Step 1: Know Your Real Take-Home Pay

Your gross income is not what hits your bank account. Calculate your actual monthly take-home pay after:
- Federal taxes
- State taxes (California has high state income tax!)
- Social Security and Medicare
- Health insurance premiums
- Retirement contributions (keep these going!)
- Any other payroll deductions

This is the real money you have to work with.

### Step 2: Account for Your Life

List out your non-negotiable monthly expenses (besides housing):
- Food and groceries
- Car payment, insurance, and gas
- Utilities and phone
- Childcare
- Health expenses not covered by insurance
- Pet expenses
- Insurance (beyond homeowners)
- Subscriptions and memberships

Now add your quality-of-life expenses:
- Dining out and entertainment
- Hobbies and activities
- Travel and vacation savings
- Kids' activities
- Shopping and personal items

And don't forget savings goals:
- Emergency fund
- Retirement (if not deducted from paycheck)
- Kids' education
- Other savings goals

### Step 3: Calculate Your Comfortable Housing Budget

**Take-home pay:** $______/month
**Minus living expenses:** $______/month
**Minus quality-of-life spending:** $______/month
**Minus savings goals:** $______/month
**= What's left for housing:** $______/month

**Important:** That number should INCLUDE:
- Mortgage principal and interest
- Property taxes
- Homeowners insurance
- Mortgage insurance (if applicable)
- HOA fees (if applicable)

### Step 4: Reality Check

Does your comfort number align with homes in your target areas?
- If yes, great! You have a realistic budget.
- If no, you have choices: save for a larger down payment, expand your search area, adjust your expectations, or wait and improve your income.

## Real Talk: California Cost of Living

California is expensive. Housing costs are high relative to much of the country. This means your housing payment might be a higher percentage of your income than the traditional 28% guideline suggests.

Many California buyers spend 30-35% or even 40% of their gross income on housing. That doesn't mean you should automatically stretch—but it does mean the traditional guidelines may not perfectly fit expensive California markets.

**The key is doing the math for YOUR situation** and being honest about what you can sustain long-term.

**Moving and setup costs:**
- Moving company or truck rental
- New furniture and appliances
- Window treatments

- Odds and ends (always more than you expect)

**Ongoing maintenance:**
- Budget 1-2% of home value per year
- For a $500,000 home = $5,000-$10,000 annually
- HVAC filters, pest control, repairs, updates

**Unexpected repairs:**
- Water heater dies
- Roof leak
- Plumbing emergency
- Appliance replacement
- The thing you never saw coming

**Pro tip:** Before you close, have at least $3,000-$5,000 set aside for immediate needs, and start building a home maintenance fund right away.

## Lesson 1: Understanding Your Loan Options

# Conventional, FHA, VA—Which Loan Is Right for You?

Choosing the right loan type can save you thousands of dollars and make the difference between qualifying and not qualifying. Let's break down the main options available to California homebuyers.

## Conventional Loans

Conventional loans are mortgages that aren't insured by a government agency. They're the most common type of loan for borrowers with good credit and solid finances.

### Conventional Loan Highlights

**Down payment:**
- Minimum 3% for first-time buyers (Fannie Mae HomeReady, Freddie Mac Home Possible)
- Minimum 5% for standard conventional loans
- 20% to avoid PMI (Private Mortgage Insurance)

**Credit score requirements:**
- Minimum: Typically 620, but 680+ preferred for best rates
- Best rates: 740+

**Mortgage insurance:**
- Required if down payment is less than 20%
- PMI costs 0.5-1.5% of loan amount annually
- **Big advantage:** PMI can be removed once you reach 20% equity!

**Loan limits (2024):**
- Standard California counties: $766,550
- High-cost counties (Los Angeles, San Francisco, San Diego, etc.): Up to $1,149,825
- Check with your lender for limits in your specific county

**Best for:**
- Borrowers with good credit (680+)
- Those who can put down 5% or more
- Buyers who want to remove PMI eventually
- Higher-priced homes in high-cost counties

### Special Conventional Programs

**Fannie Mae HomeReady:**
- Just 3% down for low-to-moderate income buyers
- Reduced PMI costs
- Can use income from family members living in home

**Freddie Mac Home Possible:**
- Also 3% down
- For low-to-moderate income buyers
- Flexible income sources allowed

## FHA Loans

FHA loans are insured by the Federal Housing Administration. They're designed to help borrowers who might not qualify for conventional financing.

### FHA Loan Highlights

**Down payment:**
- 3.5% with credit score of 580+
- 10% with credit score of 500-579

**Credit score requirements:**
- Minimum: 500 (with 10% down)
- 580+ for 3.5% down payment

**Mortgage insurance (MIP):**
- Upfront MIP: 1.75% of loan amount (can be rolled into loan)
- Annual MIP: 0.55% for most borrowers (paid monthly)
- **Important:** MIP is for the life of the loan on most FHA purchases

**Loan limits (2024):**
- Varies by county in California
- Range from $498,257 to $1,149,825
- Check the HUD website for your specific county

**Best for:**
- Borrowers with credit scores 580-680
- Those who have limited down payment savings
- Buyers who don't qualify for conventional financing
- Those recovering from credit issues

### FHA Considerations

**Pros:**
- Lower credit requirements
- Lower down payment (3.5%)
- More flexible qualification standards
- Allows higher debt-to-income ratios

**Cons:**
- Mortgage insurance for life of loan
- Upfront MIP adds to loan amount
- Property must meet FHA standards
- Loan limits may restrict options in high-cost areas

## VA Loans

VA loans are guaranteed by the Department of Veterans Affairs and available to eligible veterans, active-duty service members, and some surviving spouses.

### VA Loan Highlights

**Down payment:**
- 0% down payment!
- One of the only true no-down-payment options available

**Credit score requirements:**
- No VA minimum, but most lenders require 580-620+
- Better rates with higher scores

**Mortgage insurance:**
- No monthly mortgage insurance!
- One-time VA funding fee (can be rolled into loan)
- Funding fee waived for veterans with service-connected disabilities

**Loan limits:**
- No loan limit for borrowers with full entitlement
- Limits may apply if you have partial entitlement in use

**Best for:**
- Eligible veterans and active-duty service members
- Those who want to minimize out-of-pocket costs
- Buyers who want to avoid monthly mortgage insurance

### VA Eligibility

You may be eligible if you:
- Served 90+ days active duty during wartime
- Served 181+ days active duty during peacetime
- Served 6+ years in National Guard or Reserves
- Are the spouse of a service member who died in the line of duty

To check your eligibility, you'll need your Certificate of Eligibility (COE).

## USDA Loans

USDA loans are for homes in eligible rural areas and are backed by the U.S. Department of Agriculture.

### USDA Loan Highlights

**Down payment:**
- 0% down payment!

**Credit requirements:**
- Typically 640+ for automatic approval
- Some flexibility below 640 with manual underwriting

**Income limits:**
- Household income must be at or below 115% of area median income
- Limits vary by location and household size

**Property requirements:**
- Must be in a USDA-eligible rural area
- Some suburban areas surprisingly qualify

**Best for:**
- Buyers willing to purchase in eligible areas
- Those with limited down payment
- Moderate-income households

**California note:** USDA-eligible areas in California include many Central Valley communities, mountain/foothill areas, and rural coastal areas.

## Quick Comparison Chart

| Feature | Conventional | FHA | VA | USDA |
|---------|-------------|-----|-----|------|
| Min. Down Payment | 3-5% | 3.5% | 0% | 0% |
| Min. Credit Score | 620 (680+ preferred) | 580 | 580-620 | 640 |
| Mortgage Insurance | PMI (removable) | MIP (for life) | None (funding fee) | Annual fee |
| Income Limits | No | No | No | Yes |
| Property Requirements | Standard | Stricter | Standard | Rural location |

## Which Loan Should You Choose?

**Consider Conventional if:**
- Your credit score is 680+
- You have 5%+ for down payment
- You want to remove mortgage insurance eventually
- You're buying a higher-priced home

**Consider FHA if:**
- Your credit score is 580-680
- You have limited down payment (3.5%)
- You have some credit blemishes
- You need more flexible qualification

**Consider VA if:**
- You're an eligible veteran or active-duty member
- You want 0% down
- You want to avoid monthly mortgage insurance

**Consider USDA if:**
- You're buying in an eligible rural area
- You meet income requirements
- You want 0% down

**Pro tip:** Many buyers qualify for multiple loan types. Ask your lender to compare options so you can see which is best for your specific situation.

## Lesson 2: Interest Rate vs. APR Explained

# What's the Real Cost of Your Loan?

When you shop for a mortgage, you'll see two percentages quoted: the **interest rate** and the **APR (Annual Percentage Rate)**. They're not the same thing, and understanding the difference will help you compare loan offers accurately.

## Interest Rate: What It Is

Your interest rate is the cost of borrowing the principal (the loan amount). It's the percentage of your loan balance you pay in interest each year.

**The interest rate determines:**
- Your monthly principal and interest payment
- How much interest you'll pay each month

**Example:** On a $450,000 loan:
- At 6.5% rate: Monthly P&I = $2,844
- At 7.0% rate: Monthly P&I = $2,995
- Difference: $151/month or $1,812/year

The interest rate is the number most people focus on—and it's important! But it doesn't tell the whole story.

## APR: The Full Picture

The APR (Annual Percentage Rate) is designed to show you the true cost of the loan, including not just the interest rate but also fees and other costs.

**APR includes:**
- The interest rate
- Loan origination fees
- Discount points (if any)
- Mortgage broker fees (if applicable)
- Other lender fees

**APR does NOT typically include:**
- Third-party costs (title, escrow, etc.)
- Prepaid items (taxes, insurance)
- Private mortgage insurance

The APR spreads these costs over the loan term, giving you an annual percentage that reflects the overall cost of borrowing.

## Why Both Numbers Matter

Here's where it gets interesting—and where you can get tripped up.

**Scenario:** You're comparing two lenders:

| | Lender A | Lender B |
|---|----------|----------|
| Interest Rate | 6.75% | 6.375% |
| Points | 0 | 2 |
| Origination Fee | $1,000 | $1,500 |
| APR | 6.89% | 6.85% |

Lender B has the lower interest rate—great! But they're charging 2 points (2% of the loan amount = $9,000 on a $450,000 loan) to get that rate. When you factor in the points, Lender B's APR is very close to Lender A's.

**The question becomes:** Is the lower rate worth paying $9,000 upfront?

## Understanding Points

Points are a way to "buy down" your interest rate. Each point costs 1% of your loan amount and typically reduces your rate by about 0.25%.

**Example:** On a $450,000 loan:
- 1 point = $4,500 upfront
- Reduces rate by approximately 0.25%
- Reduces monthly payment by about $65-$75

**Break-even analysis:**
$4,500 ÷ $70/month savings = 64 months (about 5.3 years)

If you stay in the home more than 5.3 years, paying the point might make sense. If you're likely to move or refinance sooner, it doesn't.

**When paying points might make sense:**
- You plan to stay in the home 7+ years
- You have extra cash available
- You want the lowest possible monthly payment
- Interest rates are high, and you're betting on refinancing later

**When to skip points:**
- You might move in 3-5 years
- You'd rather keep cash for other purposes
- You're tight on closing costs
- You're not sure about your timeline

## Reading a Rate Quote

When you get a rate quote from a lender, make sure you understand:

1. **The interest rate** – The percentage that determines your monthly payment
2. **The APR** – The overall cost including fees
3. **Any points required** – Are they charging points to get that rate?
4. **Lender fees** – What specific fees are included?
5. **Rate lock period** – How long is this rate guaranteed?
6. **Loan type** – Is this for a 30-year fixed? 15-year? ARM?

**Pro tip:** When comparing lenders, ask for quotes at the SAME interest rate level (e.g., "What would my costs be at a 7% rate with no points?"). This lets you compare apples to apples.

## Rate Locks: Protecting Your Rate

Once you find a rate you like, you can "lock" it in. A rate lock guarantees your rate for a specific period.

**Typical lock periods:**
- 15 days: May have lowest cost
- 30 days: Standard, works for most transactions
- 45 days: For longer escrows
- 60+ days: For new construction or complex deals, usually costs more

**Important things to know:**
- Lock in writing! Verbal locks don't count.
- If rates drop after you lock, you're generally stuck (unless you have a float-down option)
- If your lock expires before closing, you may need to pay an extension fee or re-lock at current rates

## Fixed-Rate vs. Adjustable-Rate Mortgages

### Fixed-Rate Mortgages (FRM)

Your interest rate stays the same for the life of the loan.

**30-Year Fixed:**
- Most popular option
- Lowest monthly payment (longest term)
- Higher total interest paid over life of loan
- Predictable payments forever

**15-Year Fixed:**
- Higher monthly payment
- Lower interest rate (typically 0.5-0.75% less than 30-year)
- Builds equity much faster
- Pay way less total interest

### Adjustable-Rate Mortgages (ARM)

Your rate starts lower but can change after an initial fixed period.

**Common ARMs:**
- 5/1 ARM: Fixed for 5 years, then adjusts yearly
- 7/1 ARM: Fixed for 7 years, then adjusts yearly
- 10/1 ARM: Fixed for 10 years, then adjusts yearly

**Why consider an ARM?**
- Lower initial rate and payment
- Makes sense if you'll move or refinance before it adjusts
- Rate caps limit how much it can change

**Why be cautious:**
- Your payment can increase significantly
- Uncertainty about future payments
- Harder to budget long-term

**In today's market:** Most borrowers choose fixed-rate loans for the security and predictability, especially when rates aren't dramatically lower on ARMs.

## Lesson 3: How to Read Your Loan Estimate

# Your Loan Estimate: Understanding Every Number

Within three business days of applying for a mortgage, you'll receive a Loan Estimate (LE). This standardized three-page document shows you the key terms of the loan you've applied for. Understanding it is crucial for making informed decisions and comparing lenders.

Let's walk through it section by section.

## Page 1: Loan Terms and Costs Overview

The first page gives you the big picture.

### Loan Terms Box (Top Left)

**Loan Amount:** The amount you're borrowing. Verify this matches what you discussed.

**Interest Rate:** Your quoted rate. Note whether it's locked.

**Monthly Principal & Interest:** Just the P&I portion (not including taxes/insurance).

**Prepayment Penalty:** Should be "No" for most standard loans. If it says "Yes," ask questions.

**Balloon Payment:** Should be "No" for conventional mortgages. If it says "Yes," proceed with caution.

### Projected Payments Box (Middle)

This shows your complete monthly payment including:
- Principal and interest
- Mortgage insurance (if applicable)
- Estimated escrow (taxes and insurance)

**Pay attention to:**
- Whether payment changes over time (ARMs will show different phases)
- The "Estimated Total Monthly Payment" – this is the real number for budgeting
- Estimated taxes and insurance – these are estimates and can change

### Costs at Closing Box (Bottom)

**Closing Costs:** The total fees for the loan. This is the number to compare between lenders.

**Cash to Close:** The total you need to bring to closing, including down payment AND closing costs minus any credits.

## Page 2: Closing Costs Breakdown

This is where you see exactly what you're paying for.

### Section A: Origination Charges

These are fees charged by your lender.

**What you'll see:**
- Origination fee (sometimes a percentage, sometimes flat)
- Points (if you're buying down the rate)
- Application fee
- Underwriting fee

**What to compare:** This section varies most between lenders. A lender with a lower rate might have higher origination charges, making the total cost similar or higher.

### Section B: Services You Cannot Shop For

These are services required by the lender where you can't choose the provider.

**Typical items:**
- Appraisal fee ($500-$800 in California)
- Credit report fee
- Flood determination fee
- Tax monitoring fee

### Section C: Services You CAN Shop For

These are services where you have choice.

**Typical items:**
- Title insurance
- Title search
- Settlement agent/escrow fee

**Pro tip:** Your lender must give you a list of providers you can shop from. While many buyers use the lender's suggestions, you have the right to choose your own.

### Section D: Total Loan Costs

A + B + C = Total Loan Costs

### Sections E, F, G, H: Other Costs

**Section E: Taxes and Government Fees**
- Recording fees
- Transfer taxes (in some California jurisdictions)

**Section F: Prepaids**
These aren't really "costs" – they're things you'd pay anyway, just due at closing:
- Homeowners insurance premium (often first year)
- Prepaid interest (from closing date to first payment)
- Property taxes (prepaid into escrow)

**Section G: Initial Escrow Payment at Closing**
This is the amount needed to start your escrow account for taxes and insurance.

**Section H: Other**
Any other fees (owner's title insurance, etc.)

### Lender Credits

If your lender is giving you credits (to cover some closing costs in exchange for a higher rate, for example), they'll appear here as a negative number.

## Page 3: Comparisons and Other Information

### Comparisons Section

This helps you evaluate the loan:

**In 5 Years:**
- Total you'll have paid (principal, interest, mortgage insurance, loan costs)
- How much of that reduces your principal (builds equity)

This section is useful for comparing loans with different fee structures. A loan with higher upfront costs might look better or worse over 5 years.

**APR:** The Annual Percentage Rate, including costs, for comparison purposes.

**Total Interest Percentage (TIP):** The total interest you'll pay as a percentage of your loan amount. This number can be eye-opening!

### Other Considerations

**Appraisal:** Information about your right to receive a copy.

**Assumption:** Whether someone can take over your loan if you sell.

**Homeowner's Insurance:** Reminder that you need it.

**Late Payment:** What happens if you pay late.

**Servicing:** Whether your lender services the loan themselves or transfers it.

## How to Use the Loan Estimate

### Comparing Multiple Lenders

When you have Loan Estimates from multiple lenders:

1. **Compare the same loan type** – Make sure you're comparing 30-year fixed to 30-year fixed, etc.

2. **Look at total costs** – Don't just compare rates. Compare total closing costs and APR.

3. **Check lender fees** – Section A varies most. Look for excessive origination fees.

4. **Understand rate vs. cost tradeoffs** – A lower rate with higher points might cost more upfront but save money long-term.

5. **Ask questions** – If something seems high or unclear, ask for explanation.

### Checking for Changes

Your Loan Estimate may change. Some changes are normal; others are red flags.

**Can change:**
- Prepaid items (based on actual closing date)
- Services you shop for
- Escrow amounts

**Cannot increase more than 10%:**
- Recording fees
- Services you cannot shop for (combined)

**Cannot change at all:**
- Origination charges
- Transfer taxes
- Points (if rate is locked)

If your lender significantly increases fees that shouldn't change, push back or consider other options.

## Lesson 1: Income Documentation—What You'll Need

# Proving Your Income: A Complete Guide

Lenders need to verify that you have the income to support your mortgage payment. The documentation required depends on how you earn your money. Let's break it down by employment type.

## W-2 Employees: The Standard Scenario

If you work for an employer and receive a W-2 at tax time, your documentation is relatively straightforward.

### What You'll Need

**Pay stubs:**
- Most recent 30 days of pay stubs
- Should show year-to-date income
- All jobs if you have multiple employers

**W-2 forms:**
- Last 2 years
- From all employers

**Tax returns (sometimes):**
- Last 2 years of federal returns (all pages and schedules)
- Required if you have significant bonus/commission income, rental income, or other non-W-2 income

**Verification of Employment:**
- Lender will contact your employer directly
- Usually done late in the process
- Just confirms your employment status, title, dates, and income

### Tips for W-2 Employees

✅ **Keep your job stable** – Avoid changing jobs during the process if possible

✅ **Save all pay stubs** – Even digital copies work

✅ **Keep your employer informed** – Let them know someone may call to verify employment

⚠️ **Job changes:** If you're changing jobs, let your lender know. A lateral move in the same industry is often fine. A major career change might require waiting.

## Self-Employed Borrowers: More Documentation Needed

If you own a business, are an independent contractor, or are self-employed in any way, you'll need more paperwork.

### What You'll Need

**Tax returns:**
- Last 2 years of COMPLETE federal tax returns (all pages, all schedules)
- Both personal (1040) and business returns (Schedule C, 1120S, 1065, etc.)

**Year-to-date financials:**
- Profit and loss statement (current year through most recent month)
- Some lenders require a CPA-prepared statement

**Business verification:**
- Business license or DBA
- CPA letter (sometimes)
- Business bank statements (sometimes)

### How Lenders Calculate Self-Employed Income

Here's what surprises many self-employed borrowers: **lenders use your taxable income, not your gross revenue.**

All those deductions you take to reduce your tax bill? They also reduce your qualifying income for a mortgage.

**Example:**
- Your business brings in $200,000 in revenue
- After expenses and deductions, your Schedule C shows $90,000
- Your qualifying income for the mortgage is around $90,000, not $200,000

Lenders typically:
1. Add up your net income from the last 2 years of tax returns
2. Divide by 24 to get your monthly qualifying income
3. Adjust for any trends (declining income is a concern)

### Tips for Self-Employed Borrowers

✅ **Start early** – Gather your tax returns and review what they show

✅ **Talk to your lender before filing taxes** – If you're buying soon, your CPA should know

✅ **Keep business finances organized** – Clear records make everything easier

✅ **Expect more scrutiny** – It's normal; don't take it personally

⚠️ **Declining income** – If year 2 is lower than year 1, lenders will likely use the lower year

## Commission and Bonus Income

If you receive significant commissions or bonuses, here's how they're handled:

**General rule:** You need a 2-year history of commission or bonus income for it to be counted.

**Calculation:**
- Lenders average the last 2 years
- If this income is declining, they may use the lower year

**What you'll need:**
- 2 years of W-2s showing the income
- 2 years of tax returns
- Verification from employer that commission/bonus structure continues

**New to commission?** You typically need at least 12-24 months of history before it can be counted as qualifying income.

## Other Income Types

### Part-Time or Second Job Income
- Need 2-year history of holding second job
- Need likelihood of continuation

### Rental Income (from Properties You Own)
- Documented via tax returns (Schedule E)
- Typically counted at 75% of gross rents
- Operating expenses are subtracted
- Need copies of leases

### Social Security or Retirement Income
- Social Security award letter
- 2 months of bank statements showing deposits
- This income can sometimes be "grossed up" (increased) for qualification since it's often not taxed

### Child Support or Alimony
- Court order or divorce decree
- Proof it will continue for at least 3 years
- 3+ months of proof of receipt
- Bank statements showing deposits

### Investment or Dividend Income
- 2 years of tax returns
- Must show continued receipt
- Must be likely to continue

## Income Red Flags

Lenders may have concerns about:

❌ **Gaps in employment** – You'll need to explain any gaps

❌ **Job hopping** – Frequent changes can be a concern, though same-industry moves are often fine

❌ **Cash income** – If it's not on your tax returns, it doesn't count

❌ **Declining income** – A downward trend raises questions

❌ **Very recent new job** – Probationary periods, major career changes

❌ **Inconsistent income** – Wild swings year to year

## The Golden Rule of Income Documentation

**If it's not on your tax returns, it doesn't exist to a lender.**

This is why it's crucial to report all income on your taxes if you plan to buy a home. Cash income, under-the-table work, and unreported earnings cannot be used for mortgage qualification.

## Lesson 2: Asset Documentation and Sourcing

# Where's Your Money Coming From?

Lenders don't just want to know that you have money—they want to know where it came from. This is called "sourcing" your assets, and it's one of the most important (and sometimes frustrating) parts of the mortgage process.

Here's why it matters and how to make it smooth.

## Why Lenders Source Assets

Lenders source assets to:
- Prevent money laundering
- Ensure funds aren't borrowed (creating hidden debt)
- Verify you legitimately have the money for your down payment and closing costs
- Meet regulatory and investor requirements

This isn't personal—it's required for virtually every mortgage.

## What Needs to Be Sourced

**You'll need to document:**
- Down payment funds
- Closing cost funds
- Required reserves (if your loan program requires them)

All of these funds must be "seasoned" (meaning they've been in your accounts for a while) or properly documented if recently deposited.

## Bank Statement Requirements

**Standard requirement:** Last 2 months of bank statements for every account that holds funds you're using for closing.

**Important details:**
- Include ALL pages, even blank ones
- Must show your name and account number
- Must be complete monthly statements (not printouts of recent activity)

**Accounts to include:**
- Checking accounts
- Savings accounts
- Money market accounts
- Investment accounts (brokerage, etc.)

## The "Large Deposit" Rule

Here's where many buyers run into trouble: **any deposit that's more than 50% of your total monthly qualifying income must be sourced.**

**Example:**
- Your monthly income: $8,000
- Large deposit threshold: $4,000
- Any single deposit over $4,000 needs documentation

### What Counts as a Large Deposit

- Cash deposits
- Transfers from other accounts
- Checks from non-payroll sources
- Wire transfers
- Venmo, PayPal, Zelle transfers

### Documenting Large Deposits

For each large deposit, you'll need to provide:

**If it's a paycheck:**
- Pay stub matching the deposit amount

**If it's a tax refund:**
- IRS transcript or copy of tax return showing refund amount

**If it's a transfer from another account:**
- Statement from the source account showing the withdrawal

**If it's a gift:**
- Gift letter and documentation (see next lesson)

**If it's from selling something:**
- Bill of sale
- Proof of ownership
- Evidence of value

**If it's insurance proceeds:**
- Insurance company documentation

## Cash Deposits: Proceed with Caution

**Cash is the hardest thing to source.** If you deposit cash, you'll need to explain where it came from—and "I save cash at home" is very difficult to verify.

**Best practices:**
- Avoid large cash deposits once you start the mortgage process
- If you must deposit cash, keep it under the large deposit threshold
- Be prepared to explain any cash deposits

**Example problems:**
- "I sold my car for cash" – Need bill of sale, title transfer, proof of value
- "It's savings from around the house" – Very difficult to document
- "Someone paid me back money they owed me" – Very difficult to document

## Moving Money Between Accounts

Every time you transfer money between accounts, it creates a paper trail that needs to be documented.

**The rule:** You need to show both sides of the transfer—the withdrawal from one account and the deposit to another.

**Best practice:** Once you start the mortgage process, don't move money around unnecessarily. Keep funds in the accounts where they are.

**If you must consolidate funds:**
- Do it before you apply if possible
- Keep statements from all source accounts
- Make sure the amounts match

## Gift Funds (See Next Lesson for Full Details)

Using gift money for your down payment? You'll need:
- Gift letter signed by the donor
- Proof the donor has the funds (their bank statement)
- Proof the funds were transferred
- Paper trail from their account to yours

## Retirement Accounts

You may be able to use retirement funds for your down payment:

**401(k) loans:**
- Borrow against your 401(k)
- The loan payment must be included in your debt-to-income ratio
- Need plan documents showing terms

**401(k) withdrawals:**
- Early withdrawal may incur penalties and taxes
- Need statement showing the funds

**IRA withdrawals:**
- First-time homebuyer exception allows up to $10,000 penalty-free from traditional IRA
- Roth contributions can be withdrawn anytime
- Need statement showing availability

**For all retirement accounts:**
- Provide most recent quarterly statement
- Document any withdrawals

## Required Reserves

Some loan programs require you to have reserves—money left over after you close.

**Typical reserve requirements:**
- Conventional: 0-6 months depending on factors
- Jumbo loans: Often 6-12 months
- Investment properties: Usually required

**What counts as reserves:**
- Bank accounts
- Investment accounts
- Retirement accounts (typically 60% of value counted)

## Common Asset Documentation Mistakes

❌ **Moving money right before applying** – Creates sourcing headaches

❌ **Large cash deposits** – Very hard to source

❌ **Not keeping all statement pages** – Every page is required

❌ **Using cryptocurrency** – Most lenders require it converted and seasoned

❌ **Receiving money without a paper trail** – Everything needs documentation

❌ **Unexplained large purchases** – Money going OUT also matters

- [ ] Review statements for any large deposits and gather documentation
- [ ] Stop making large cash deposits until after closing
- [ ] Avoid moving money between accounts unnecessarily
- [ ] If using gift funds, start gift letter process early (see next lesson)
- [ ] If using retirement funds, get statements and understand withdrawal rules
- [ ] Keep all pages of every statement, even blank ones

## Lesson 3: Using Gift Funds—The Complete Guide

# Getting Help from Family: How Gift Funds Work

Good news: using gift money for your down payment is absolutely allowed and very common! But there are specific rules and documentation requirements you need to follow.

## What Qualifies as a Gift

For mortgage purposes, a gift must be:
- Truly a gift with no expectation of repayment
- From an acceptable donor
- Properly documented

**Important:** If you're expected to pay the money back, it's a loan, not a gift—and it has to be treated completely differently.

## Who Can Give You a Gift?

### Always Acceptable Donors:
- Parents and step-parents
- Grandparents
- Siblings
- Spouse or domestic partner
- Aunts, uncles, and cousins
- Children (if they have the means)
- Legal guardian

### Sometimes Acceptable:
- Close family friends (with additional documentation)
- Employer (may be subject to conditions)
- Fiancé/fiancée (varies by lender)

### Not Acceptable:
- The seller of the home
- The real estate agent
- The builder (in most cases)
- The lender
- Any interested party to the transaction
- Anonymous donors

## How Much Can You Receive?

This depends on your loan type and down payment:

**Conventional Loans:**
- If putting down 20%+: 100% of down payment can be a gift
- If putting down less than 20%: At least 5% typically must be your own funds for primary residence (rules vary)
- For second homes/investment: Stricter requirements

**FHA Loans:**
- 100% of down payment can be a gift
- Must be from a family member

**VA Loans:**
- 100% can be gift (not that you need a down payment!)
- More flexible on donor requirements

**Always confirm the specific gift requirements with your lender.**

## The Gift Letter

You'll need a gift letter signed by the donor. It must include:

**Required information:**
1. Donor's name, address, and phone number
2. Donor's relationship to you
3. Dollar amount of the gift
4. Property address (or "for home purchase" if address not yet known)
5. Statement that NO repayment is expected or required
6. Donor's signature and date

**Sample gift letter language:**

*"I/We, [Donor Name], am gifting the sum of $[Amount] to [Borrower Name], who is my [relationship], for the purchase of the property located at [Address or 'to be determined']. This is a bona fide gift with no expectation or requirement of repayment. These funds do not represent a loan and are given freely."*

*[Donor signature and date]*

## Documenting the Gift

Beyond the letter, you'll need:

**From the donor:**
- Bank statement showing they have the funds available
- Documentation of the transfer (wire confirmation, check copy)

**From you:**
- Bank statement showing receipt of the gift
- The funds should be identifiable (similar amount, similar timing)

## Best Practices for Gift Fund Timing

**Option 1: Gift Before Application**
- Receive gift at least 2 months before applying
- It will be "seasoned" in your account
- Simpler documentation (just your statements showing the balance)

**Option 2: Gift During the Transaction**
- More common, but requires more documentation
- Keep all transfer records
- Get donor's statement showing the withdrawal
- Paper trail must be clear

**Option 3: Gift at Closing**
- Donor wires funds directly to escrow
- Need gift letter in advance
- Need donor's statement showing they have the funds
- Wire must come from documented donor

## Multiple Gifts

Yes, you can receive gifts from multiple family members. Each donor needs:
- Their own gift letter
- Their own bank statement
- Documentation of their transfer

## What NOT to Do with Gift Funds

❌ **Don't have gifts deposited as cash** – Creates sourcing nightmares

❌ **Don't commingle with other funds immediately** – Keep it trackable

❌ **Don't accept gifts from unacceptable donors** – Even if they want to help

❌ **Don't try to disguise a loan as a gift** – This is fraud

❌ **Don't have the donor write a check to you from their credit card** – That's borrowed money

❌ **Don't accept gifts from overseas without extra documentation** – Foreign gifts require additional verification

## Gift Funds from Overseas

If your gift is coming from family overseas, expect extra documentation:
- Proof of relationship
- Proof of donor's income/assets
- Wire transfer documentation
- Currency conversion records
- Possibly a longer paper trail

Plan extra time for international gifts.

- [ ] Verify that your donor(s) are acceptable sources
- [ ] Have donor prepare gift letter with all required elements
- [ ] Obtain a copy of donor's most recent bank statement
- [ ] Coordinate timing of gift transfer
- [ ] Keep all documentation of the transfer
- [ ] Save your bank statement showing receipt of funds

## Lesson 1: Types of Down Payment Assistance

# Down Payment Assistance: Getting Help to Buy Your Home

Here's something many first-time buyers don't realize: **you don't have to come up with the entire down payment yourself.** California has numerous programs designed to help buyers—especially first-time and moderate-income buyers—get into homes.

Let's explore what's available.

## What Is Down Payment Assistance (DPA)?

Down payment assistance programs provide funds to help cover your down payment and sometimes closing costs. They come from various sources:
- State programs (CalHFA)
- National programs (GSFA and others)
- Local/city programs (varies by location)
- Employer programs

## Types of DPA Programs

### Silent Second Mortgages

**How they work:**
- You receive a second loan for your down payment
- No monthly payments are required
- The loan is "deferred" until you sell, refinance, or pay off the first mortgage

**The "silent" part:** You don't make payments on this loan—it just sits there until a triggering event.

**Repayment:**
- When you sell the home
- When you refinance the first mortgage
- When you pay off the first mortgage
- Sometimes after a certain number of years

**Typical terms:**
- 0% interest (or low interest)
- No payments during the silent period
- Full amount due at the triggering event

**Who it's for:**
- Buyers who need help with down payment
- Those who may move or refinance within 5-10 years
- Those comfortable with deferred repayment

### Forgivable Loans

**How they work:**
- You receive a second loan for your down payment
- No monthly payments required
- A portion (or all) of the loan is forgiven over time

**Forgiveness schedule:**
- Often forgiven over 3-10 years
- May be forgiven monthly or annually
- Typically prorated if you trigger repayment early

**Example:**
- $15,000 forgivable loan over 5 years
- $3,000 forgiven each year
- If you sell after 3 years, you've had $9,000 forgiven and owe $6,000

**Who it's for:**
- Buyers who plan to stay in the home for several years
- Those who want potential "free money" if they stay long enough
- Buyers willing to commit to the home as a primary residence

### Grants

**How they work:**
- True gift money that never needs to be repaid
- Typically smaller amounts than loans
- Often have stricter eligibility requirements

**No repayment:** Ever. If you get a grant, it's yours—no strings attached (other than using it for its intended purpose).

**Who it's for:**
- Buyers who meet specific eligibility requirements
- Often tied to income limits, first-time buyer status, or specific professions
- Those who can act quickly (grants often have limited funding)

### Repayable Second Mortgages

**How they work:**
- Standard second mortgage with monthly payments
- Typically at below-market interest rates
- Adds to your monthly housing payment

**Monthly payment impact:**
- Unlike silent seconds, you DO make payments
- Must qualify for both loans' payments

**Who it's for:**
- Buyers who can afford a slightly higher total payment
- Those who don't qualify for deferred payment programs
- Those who want to build equity in both loans

## How DPA Affects Your First Mortgage

Most DPA programs require you to use a specific first mortgage product or lender. This means:

**Interest rates:** May be slightly higher than market rates to accommodate the DPA

**Loan types:** Often FHA or specific conventional products

**Lender requirements:** Must work with an approved DPA lender

**The trade-off is worth it:** Even with a slightly higher rate, the DPA can make buying possible when it otherwise wouldn't be. Always have your lender show you comparisons.

## Lesson 2: California's Major DPA Programs

# CalHFA, GSFA, and Other California Programs

California has some of the best down payment assistance programs in the country. Let's explore the major options available to you.

## CalHFA MyHome Assistance Program

The California Housing Finance Agency (CalHFA) offers the MyHome Assistance Program, one of the state's most popular DPA options.

**Program overview:**
- Provides assistance for down payment and/or closing costs
- Deferred payment second mortgage
- Available with CalHFA first mortgage programs

**How much assistance:**
- Up to 3.5% of the purchase price or appraised value (whichever is less)

**Program type:**
- Silent second mortgage
- Simple interest
- No monthly payments
- Due when you sell, refinance, or pay off the first mortgage

**Eligibility basics:**
- Must be a first-time homebuyer (haven't owned in past 3 years)
- Must occupy the home as your primary residence
- Must meet income limits for your county
- Must complete homebuyer education
- Property must be in California

**First mortgage options:**
- CalHFA FHA loan
- CalHFA conventional loan
- CalPLUS programs

**Check with your lender for current income limits, purchase price limits, and specific eligibility requirements in your county.**

## CalHFA ZIP (Zero Interest Program)

This is a zero-interest down payment assistance option through CalHFA.

**Program overview:**
- Zero percent interest rate
- Deferred payment second mortgage
- Designed to work with CalPLUS first mortgage products

**How much assistance:**
- Up to 3% of the first mortgage amount for closing costs
- Combined with CalPLUS for a complete financing solution

**Program type:**
- Silent second mortgage
- Zero interest
- No monthly payments
- Due when you sell, refinance, or pay off first mortgage

**How it works:**
- CalPLUS programs have a slightly higher interest rate
- In exchange, you get the ZIP assistance at 0%
- This covers closing costs, making the transaction more affordable

**Check with your lender for current availability and eligibility requirements.**

## GSFA (Golden State Finance Authority)

GSFA is a national program that's widely available in California through participating lenders.

**Program overview:**
- Down payment and closing cost assistance
- Works with FHA, VA, USDA, and conventional loans
- Available through approved lenders statewide

**How much assistance:**
- Up to 5.5% of the loan amount (varies by program tier)

**Program types:**
- Forgivable option (may be forgiven over time if you stay in the home)
- Repayable option (second mortgage with payments)
- Different terms depending on qualifying factors

**Eligibility basics:**
- May be available to first-time AND repeat buyers (depending on program)
- Must meet income limits
- Must occupy as primary residence
- Must complete homebuyer education
- Minimum credit scores apply

**Key advantages:**
- Available to repeat buyers in some cases
- Works with multiple loan types
- Relatively flexible compared to some programs

**Check with your lender for current GSFA programs, income limits, and eligibility requirements.**

## City of Los Angeles MIPA (Mortgage Insurance Premium Assistance)

For buyers purchasing in the City of Los Angeles, the MIPA program may offer additional help.

**Program overview:**
- Provides assistance with mortgage costs for LA buyers
- Limited funding—not always available
- Specific to City of Los Angeles properties

**Important notes:**
- Funding is limited and may be exhausted
- Check current availability before counting on this program
- Must meet City of LA requirements

**Check with your lender for current MIPA availability, as funding status changes frequently.**

## County and Local Programs

Beyond statewide programs, many California counties and cities offer their own assistance:

**Examples may include:**
- County-specific down payment programs
- First-time buyer programs through local housing authorities
- Closing cost assistance grants

These programs change frequently and have varying availability. Your lender can help identify what's available in your specific purchase area.

| Terms | What triggers repayment? How is forgiveness calculated? |
| Rate impact | How does this affect my first mortgage rate? |
| Eligibility | Do I meet income, credit, and buyer status requirements? |
| Availability | Is the program currently funded? |

## Lesson 3: DPA Eligibility Requirements

# Do You Qualify for Down Payment Assistance?

While each program has specific requirements, there are common eligibility factors across most California DPA programs. Let's break them down.

## First-Time Homebuyer Requirement

**Definition varies but typically means:**
- You have not owned a home in the past 3 years
- You have not owned a home EVER (for some programs)

**Exceptions may apply:**
- Displaced homemakers
- Single parents who only owned with a former spouse
- Those who owned a home not permanently affixed to a foundation

**Repeat buyers:**
- Some programs (like certain GSFA tiers) ARE available to repeat buyers
- Usually with different terms or limits

**How it's verified:**
- Tax returns (mortgage interest deduction)
- Your attestation on the application
- Title history in some cases

## Income Limits

Most DPA programs have maximum income limits based on:
- Your county of purchase
- Your household size
- Area Median Income (AMI) for that location

**How household income is calculated:**
- All adult household members' income may count
- Include salary, wages, bonuses, self-employment, etc.
- Some unearned income may be included

**Typical limits:**
- Many programs cap at 80%, 100%, 120%, or 150% of AMI
- This varies significantly by program and county
- High-cost counties like San Francisco have higher dollar limits

**Example (hypothetical, for illustration):**
- County AMI for 4-person household: $100,000
- Program at 120% AMI: $120,000 max household income
- Same program at 80% AMI: $80,000 max household income

**Your lender will verify current income limits for your specific situation and county.**

## Credit Score Requirements

**Typical minimums:**
- CalHFA programs: 660-680+ depending on loan type
- GSFA programs: 620-640+ depending on tier
- Other programs: Varies

Higher credit scores may qualify for:
- Better terms
- More program options
- Higher assistance amounts

## Property Requirements

**Common requirements:**

**Location:**
- Property must be in California (for state programs)
- Some programs are county or city-specific

**Property type:**
- Single-family homes: Almost always eligible
- Condos/townhomes: Usually eligible if they meet guidelines
- 2-4 units: May be eligible if you occupy one unit
- Manufactured homes: Varies by program

**Purchase price limits:**
- Many programs have maximum purchase price or loan limits
- Varies by county
- Higher limits in high-cost areas

**Occupancy:**
- Must be your primary residence
- Cannot be an investment or second home
- Must occupy within 60 days of closing (typically)

## Homebuyer Education Requirement

**Almost all DPA programs require homebuyer education.**

**Options include:**
- HUD-approved homebuyer education course
- Online courses (often 6-8 hours)
- In-person courses
- Must complete BEFORE closing

**What you'll learn:**
- Budgeting and financial management
- The homebuying process
- Maintaining your home
- Avoiding foreclosure

**Certificate:**
- You'll receive a certificate upon completion
- This is required for your loan file
- Keep a copy for your records

**Where to find courses:**
- Ask your lender for recommendations
- HUD website has approved counselors
- Many are free or low-cost

## Loan Requirements

**DPA programs typically require:**

**Specific first mortgage products:**
- Must use the program's approved first mortgage
- CalHFA DPA requires CalHFA first mortgage, for example

**Loan type:**
- Many programs work with FHA loans
- Some work with conventional loans
- VA and USDA compatibility varies

**Interest rate considerations:**
- DPA programs may have slightly higher rates than market
- This is how the assistance is funded
- Compare the total cost, not just the rate

## Layering Programs

**Can you combine multiple DPA sources?**
- Sometimes yes, sometimes no
- Each program has its own layering rules
- Your lender can advise on compatible combinations

**Example of layering:**
- CalHFA MyHome (for down payment)
- CalHFA ZIP (for closing costs)
- Used together for a complete solution

## Lesson 4: Choosing the Right DPA Program

# Which Down Payment Assistance Is Best for You?

With multiple programs available, how do you choose? The right program depends on your unique situation. Let's work through the decision.

## Key Questions to Consider

### How long do you plan to stay in this home?

**If you might move in 3-5 years:**
- Silent second mortgages may work well
- You'll repay when you sell, but you had use of the funds
- Forgivable loans may not have time to fully forgive

**If you plan to stay 5-10+ years:**
- Forgivable loans become more attractive
- More of the assistance becomes truly "free"
- Long-term rate differences matter more

**If you're not sure:**
- Consider the worst case—what if you have to move?
- Model out different scenarios with your lender

### How much assistance do you need?

**Just need help with down payment:**
- Standard DPA programs work great
- 3-5% of purchase price usually covers it

**Need help with closing costs too:**
- Look for programs that cover both
- CalPLUS with ZIP is designed for this
- Higher-tier GSFA programs may help

**Have significant savings but want to preserve them:**
- DPA lets you keep reserves for emergencies
- May be strategic even if you could put more down

### What's your credit score?

**Higher credit (680+):**
- More programs available
- May qualify for better terms
- Conventional options usually open

**Moderate credit (620-679):**
- FHA-based programs typically available
- Some conventional options may work
- GSFA has various tiers

**Credit below 620:**
- Options are limited
- May need to improve credit first
- Talk to your lender about the path forward

### What rate trade-off can you accept?

Most DPA programs come with slightly higher interest rates. Consider:

- **The monthly difference:** How much more per month for the DPA rate vs. market rate?
- **The benefit received:** How much DPA are you getting?
- **The break-even point:** How long until the rate difference "costs" you the DPA amount?

**Example calculation (hypothetical):**
- DPA assistance: $15,000
- Rate increase: 0.25% higher
- Extra monthly cost: $60/month
- Break-even: $15,000 ÷ $60 = 250 months (20+ years)

In this example, the DPA is a clear win unless you're refinancing or selling within just a few years.

## Comparing Specific Programs

**Ask your lender to provide:**

1. **Side-by-side comparison** of available programs
2. **Total monthly payment** under each option
3. **Cash needed to close** under each option
4. **Terms of the DPA** (forgiveness schedule, repayment triggers)
5. **Total cost over 5 years and 10 years**

## Common Scenarios

### Scenario A: First-Time Buyer with Limited Savings

**Situation:** $50,000 in savings, buying a $500,000 home
- 3% down payment needed: $15,000
- Closing costs (3%): $15,000
- Reserves after: $20,000

**Without DPA:** Tight on reserves after closing

**With DPA:** Keep more savings for emergencies, moving costs, etc.

**Best fit:** Maximum DPA available to preserve reserves

### Scenario B: Moderate Income, Good Credit, Plan to Stay Long-Term

**Situation:** Stable job, 700+ credit, buying a "forever home"

**Best fit:** Forgivable loan program
- Take advantage of forgiveness over time
- Plan to be there long enough for significant forgiveness
- Lower urgency around rate differences

### Scenario C: Higher Income, Might Move in 5 Years

**Situation:** Growing career, possible relocation, but want to stop renting

**Best fit:** Silent second mortgage or minimal DPA
- Use what you need
- Understand repayment triggers
- Model the scenarios

## Application Tips

**When applying for DPA:**

✅ **Apply early** – Some programs have limited funds

✅ **Have documents ready** – Income verification moves faster with organization

✅ **Complete homebuyer education** – Do this ASAP; it's required

✅ **Work with an experienced lender** – DPA has nuances; experience helps

✅ **Ask questions** – Make sure you understand repayment terms

✅ **Get it in writing** – Understand exactly what you're receiving

## When DPA Might Not Make Sense

DPA isn't for everyone. Consider skipping it if:

- You have significant savings and want the lowest possible rate
- The rate difference outweighs the benefit for your timeline
- You're buying above program price limits
- You don't meet eligibility requirements
- You prefer a simpler loan structure

**There's no shame in not using DPA** if it doesn't fit your situation.

## Lesson 3: Protecting Yourself with Contingencies

# Contingencies: Your Safety Net

Contingencies are clauses in your purchase contract that allow you to back out (and get your earnest money back) if certain conditions aren't met. They're your protection against buying a money pit or losing your deposit.

## The Major Contingencies

### Inspection Contingency

**What it is:**
Your right to have the property professionally inspected and negotiate or cancel based on findings.

**Standard period:** 17 days in California

**What happens:**
1. You hire inspectors (see Module 7 for details)
2. Inspections reveal the home's condition
3. You can request repairs, ask for credits, accept as-is, or cancel

**When to cancel:**
- Major issues discovered (foundation, roof, systems)
- Costs to repair exceed what you're comfortable with
- Issues that can't be adequately fixed

**If you cancel within the contingency period:** You get your earnest money back.

### Appraisal Contingency

**What it is:**
Your protection if the home appraises for less than your purchase price.

**Why it matters:**
Your lender will only lend based on the appraised value. If the home appraises low, you'd need to:
- Make up the difference in cash
- Renegotiate the price with the seller
- Walk away

**With appraisal contingency:** You can cancel or renegotiate if the appraisal comes in low.

**Without appraisal contingency:** You're committed to the purchase price, even if appraisal is low. You must cover the gap with cash.

### Loan Contingency

**What it is:**
Your protection if your financing falls through.

**Standard period:** 21 days in California

**Situations it covers:**
- Your loan is denied
- Terms change unfavorably
- Lender requirements not met

**With loan contingency:** If you can't get financing, you can cancel and get your deposit back.

**Without loan contingency:** If your loan falls through, you may lose your earnest money.

## Waiving Contingencies: Risk vs. Reward

In competitive markets, some buyers waive contingencies to strengthen their offers. **This is risky and should be done with full understanding of the consequences.**

### Waiving Inspection Contingency

**The risk:** You might buy a home with serious hidden problems.

**Mitigation strategies:**
- Pre-inspection before making an offer (if seller allows)
- Cap on credit request instead of waiving entirely
- Keep a "for informational purposes" inspection

**Should you waive?** Only in very competitive situations, and only if you have cash reserves to handle surprises.

### Waiving Appraisal Contingency

**The risk:** Paying more than market value and needing extra cash at closing.

**Mitigation strategies:**
- Partial waiver (you'll cover up to $X gap)
- Strong research on comparable sales
- Significant cash reserves

**Should you waive?** Only if you have cash to cover a gap and are comfortable potentially paying above appraised value.

### Waiving Loan Contingency

**The risk:** Losing your earnest money if financing falls through.

**When it's less risky:**
- You're paying all cash (no financing needed)
- You're fully underwritten, not just pre-approved
- You have backup financing options

**Should you waive?** Rarely. This carries significant financial risk.

## California's Contingency Removal Process

In California, contingencies are actively removed (not just expired). Here's how it works:

### The Timeline

- Contract specifies contingency periods (e.g., 17 days for inspections)
- Before the period ends, you must remove the contingency in writing
- If you don't remove, the contingency remains active (but you're expected to act in good faith)

### The Forms

- Your agent will prepare a Contingency Removal form (CR)
- You sign and submit before the deadline
- This signals you're moving forward

### Important Note

**Removing a contingency means giving up that protection.** Only remove when you're satisfied:
- Inspection contingency: Remove after inspections are complete and you're satisfied
- Appraisal contingency: Remove after appraisal is received (or waive upfront)
- Loan contingency: Remove only after final loan approval

## Balancing Protection and Competitiveness

**The safest approach:**
Keep all contingencies and standard timeframes. You're fully protected.

**The most competitive approach:**
Shorten or waive contingencies. You might win more offers but take on more risk.

**The balanced approach:**
- Shorten contingency periods (e.g., 10 days instead of 17)
- Limit repair requests to major items
- Show flexibility while maintaining core protections

**Work with your agent to find the right balance for the specific property and competition level.**

## Lesson 1: Earnest Money and Opening Escrow

# Your Offer Was Accepted—Now What?

Congratulations! Your offer has been accepted, and you're officially "in contract." This is an exciting moment, but the work isn't over. In fact, one of the most important phases of the homebuying process is just beginning: escrow.

Let's walk through exactly what happens next, starting with your earnest money deposit and the opening of escrow.

## What Is Escrow, Exactly?

Think of escrow as a neutral referee. An escrow company (or sometimes an attorney) acts as a trusted third party that holds funds and documents while the buyer, seller, and lender complete all the steps needed to close the deal.

**The escrow company:**
- Holds your earnest money deposit safely
- Coordinates with the lender, title company, real estate agents, and both parties
- Ensures all conditions of the contract are met before releasing funds
- Prepares the final closing documents
- Records the deed transfer with the county

In California, escrow is typically handled by an independent escrow company or a title company's escrow division. Unlike some states where attorneys handle closings, California uses this neutral third-party system.

## Earnest Money: Your "Good Faith" Deposit

### What Is Earnest Money?

Your earnest money deposit (EMD) is a sum of money you put down to show the seller you're serious about buying their home. It's sometimes called a "good faith deposit" because it demonstrates your commitment to following through on the purchase.

**Here's what you need to know:**
- It's NOT an extra cost—it's applied to your down payment and/or closing costs at closing
- It IS at risk if you breach the contract (but contingencies protect you)
- It shows the seller you have skin in the game

### How Much Should You Deposit?

In California, earnest money typically ranges from **1% to 3% of the purchase price**, though there's no legal requirement for a specific amount.

**Examples:**

| Home Price | 1% EMD | 2% EMD | 3% EMD |
|-----------|--------|--------|--------|
| $400,000 | $4,000 | $8,000 | $12,000 |
| $600,000 | $6,000 | $12,000 | $18,000 |
| $800,000 | $8,000 | $16,000 | $24,000 |

**In competitive situations**, a larger earnest money deposit can strengthen your offer. It signals to the seller that you're financially capable and committed. Some buyers in very hot markets deposit 3% or more.

### When and How to Deposit

- **Timing:** You typically have **3 business days** after the seller accepts your offer to deposit your earnest money (though your contract may specify a different timeline)
- **Method:** Personal check, cashier's check, or wire transfer to the escrow company
- **Important:** NEVER wire money based on emailed instructions without verifying by phone (we'll cover wire fraud in the next lesson)

### When Do You Get It Back?

Your earnest money is protected by your contingencies. If you cancel within your contingency periods (inspection, appraisal, loan), you'll get your full deposit back.

**You may LOSE your earnest money if:**
- You cancel after removing all contingencies (without a valid reason)
- You breach the contract terms
- You simply change your mind after contingencies are removed

**You will GET your earnest money back if:**
- You cancel within an active contingency period
- The seller breaches the contract
- Both parties agree to cancel

### If There's a Dispute

If buyer and seller disagree about who gets the earnest money, California law requires that escrow hold the funds until both parties agree or a court decides. This can sometimes take months to resolve, which is why it's important to understand your contingency deadlines.

## Opening Escrow: The Process

### What Happens When Escrow Opens

Once your offer is accepted, your agent will "open escrow" by sending the executed purchase agreement to the escrow company. Here's the sequence:

**Day 1-3: Initial Setup**
- Escrow company receives the purchase agreement
- An escrow officer is assigned to your file
- Escrow instructions are prepared and sent to both parties
- You deposit your earnest money

**Week 1: Getting Organized**
- Title search is ordered (to ensure clear ownership)
- Your lender is notified and begins the loan process
- You schedule inspections
- Preliminary title report is issued

**Weeks 2-4: Due Diligence**
- Inspections completed
- Appraisal ordered and completed
- Loan processing and underwriting
- Contingencies addressed and removed

**Final Week: Closing Preparation**
- Final loan approval ("clear to close")
- Closing Disclosure reviewed (3-day waiting period)
- Final walkthrough
- Signing appointment
- Funding and recording

### Your Escrow Officer: Your New Best Friend

Your escrow officer manages the entire process. They'll:
- Send you escrow instructions to sign
- Request documents and information as needed
- Coordinate with your lender on timing
- Prepare the settlement statement showing all costs
- Schedule your signing appointment
- Handle the recording of the deed

**Tips for working with your escrow officer:**
1. Save their phone number and email
2. Respond to requests within 24 hours (faster is better)
3. Read everything before signing—ask questions if anything is unclear
4. Keep them informed of any changes (closing date, name on title, etc.)
5. Be patient but proactive—don't hesitate to check in on progress

## Critical Rules During Escrow

**DO NOT do any of the following while you're in escrow:**

❌ **Make large purchases** (cars, furniture, appliances on credit)
❌ **Open new credit accounts** (credit cards, personal loans)
❌ **Close existing credit accounts**
❌ **Change jobs** or quit your job
❌ **Make large cash deposits** without documentation
❌ **Co-sign for anyone**
❌ **Move money between accounts** without telling your lender first

**Why?** Your lender will re-verify your finances before closing. Any changes to your credit, income, or assets can delay or even kill your loan approval. That new car might feel urgent, but it can wait until after you close.

## The Escrow Timeline in California

Most California escrows close in **30-45 days**, though some situations take longer:

| Scenario | Typical Timeline |
|----------|-----------------|
| Conventional loan | 30-35 days |
| FHA loan | 35-45 days |
| VA loan | 35-45 days |
| DPA programs | 45-60 days |
| Cash purchase | 14-21 days |
| New construction | Varies widely |

Your specific timeline depends on your loan type, the lender's processing speed, and any issues that arise during the process.

## Lesson 2: Wire Fraud Prevention

# Protecting Your Money: Wire Fraud Is Real

This might be the most important lesson in this entire course. Wire fraud targeting homebuyers is one of the fastest-growing crimes in real estate, and the losses are devastating—often your entire down payment, gone in minutes with little chance of recovery.

This isn't meant to scare you. It's meant to prepare you so it never happens to you.

## How Wire Fraud Works

Here's a typical scenario:

1. **The setup:** Criminals hack into an email account—yours, your agent's, your escrow officer's, or your lender's. They often do this weeks before closing and quietly monitor emails to learn about your transaction.

2. **The timing:** A day or two before closing, when you're expecting wire instructions, the criminal sends an email that looks exactly like it's from your escrow company. Same logo, similar email address, professional formatting.

3. **The trap:** The email contains wire instructions—but the account number belongs to the criminal, not the escrow company.

4. **The theft:** You wire your down payment and closing costs (often $50,000-$200,000+) to the criminal's account.

5. **The aftermath:** Within minutes, the money is moved to other accounts, often overseas. By the time you realize what happened, the money is gone.

**Real statistics:**
- The FBI reports over **$400 million** in real estate wire fraud losses annually
- The average loss per victim is **over $150,000**
- Recovery rates are extremely low—especially if not caught within hours

## Recognizing the Warning Signs

### Red Flags in Emails

🚩 Wire instructions sent via email (even if they look legitimate)
🚩 Last-minute changes to previously provided wire instructions
🚩 Urgent language: "Wire must be sent today" or "Account has changed"
🚩 Email address that's slightly different (e.g., john@titlecompany.com vs. john@titIecompany.com—notice the capital I replacing the lowercase L)
🚩 Typos, odd formatting, or slightly different logos
🚩 Instructions from someone other than your escrow officer

### Red Flags in Process

🚩 Being asked to wire to a personal account
🚩 Being told to keep the wire transfer confidential
🚩 Receiving instructions from a different phone number
🚩 Being pressured to wire immediately without verification

## Your Wire Fraud Prevention Checklist

### Before Closing

**Step 1: Establish verification procedures early**
- At the start of escrow, ask your escrow officer how they send wire instructions
- Get a direct phone number for verification (from a business card or known source—NOT from an email)
- Ask if they use a secure portal for wire instructions

**Step 2: Set up email security**
- Enable two-factor authentication on your email
- Change your email password
- Don't discuss financial details over unsecured email

### When You Receive Wire Instructions

**Step 3: ALWAYS verify by phone**
- Call your escrow company using a phone number you already have (from their website, your original paperwork, or a business card)
- NEVER call a number provided in the email with wire instructions
- Verbally confirm every digit of the account number and routing number

**Step 4: Verify at your bank**
- Consider wiring in person at your bank
- Ask the bank to verify the receiving account name matches the escrow company
- Some banks can hold wires briefly before releasing—ask about this option

**Step 5: Send a test wire first (if possible)**
- Some buyers send a small amount first ($100) and verify receipt before sending the full amount
- Not always possible given timing, but worth asking about

### After Wiring

**Step 6: Confirm receipt**
- Call your escrow officer (at the known number) to confirm they received the wire
- Get confirmation the same day
- Don't assume it arrived—verify

## What to Do If You Suspect Fraud

**Time is critical. Act within MINUTES, not hours.**

**Immediately (within the first 30 minutes):**
1. **Call your bank** and request they recall the wire transfer
2. **Call your escrow company** to alert them
3. **Call your real estate agent** and lender

**Within the first few hours:**
4. **File a complaint with the FBI** at ic3.gov (Internet Crime Complaint Center)
5. **File a police report** with your local law enforcement
6. **Contact your bank's fraud department** for ongoing investigation

**The golden window:** If you act within 24-72 hours, there's a better chance of recovering some or all of the funds through the bank's recall process. After that, chances drop dramatically.

## Your Escrow Company's Responsibilities

Reputable escrow companies in California will:
- **Never send wire instructions by email alone** (they should use secure portals or provide them in person/by phone)
- **Have security protocols** for verifying identity before discussing wire details
- **Call you before closing** to confirm wire amounts and timing
- **Post wire fraud warnings** on their communications
- **Use encrypted communications** for sensitive information

If your escrow company only sends wire instructions via email with no other verification, consider that a red flag.

## The Bottom Line

**One phone call can save your life savings.** Before you wire any money during the homebuying process, pick up the phone and verify. Every. Single. Time.

## Lesson 3: Appraisal and Underwriting

# The Appraisal and Underwriting Process

You're in escrow, your inspections are done, and things are moving forward. Now comes two critical steps that happen mostly behind the scenes: the appraisal and underwriting. These determine whether your lender will actually fund your loan—and they can make or break your deal.

Let's demystify both processes so you know exactly what to expect.

## The Appraisal: What Is Your Home Really Worth?

### Why Lenders Require an Appraisal

Your lender is investing hundreds of thousands of dollars in your home purchase. They need to make sure the property is worth at least what you're paying for it. The appraisal is their independent verification.

**Think of it this way:** If you stop making payments and the lender has to foreclose, they need to know they can sell the property for enough to recover their investment.

### How the Appraisal Process Works

**Step 1: Your lender orders the appraisal**
After you're in contract, your lender orders an appraisal through an Appraisal Management Company (AMC). You don't get to choose the appraiser—this ensures independence.

**Step 2: The appraiser visits the property**
The appraiser will:
- Inspect the property inside and out
- Measure the square footage
- Note the condition, features, and any improvements
- Take photos
- Evaluate the neighborhood and location

**Step 3: The appraiser researches comparable sales**
They'll find 3-6 recent sales of similar homes in the area ("comps") and adjust for differences:
- Size differences
- Number of bedrooms/bathrooms
- Lot size
- Condition and upgrades
- Age of home
- Location within the neighborhood

**Step 4: The appraiser delivers the report**
The appraisal report includes:
- The appraised value
- Description of the property
- Comparable sales used
- Photos of the property and comps
- Any conditions or concerns noted

**Timeline:** From ordering to receiving the report typically takes **7-14 days** in California, though it can be faster or slower depending on appraiser availability.

**Cost:** Appraisals typically cost **$450-$750** in California, sometimes more for complex properties or rural areas. You pay this fee (usually at closing or upfront).

### The Three Possible Outcomes

**Scenario 1: Appraisal at or above purchase price** ✅
This is what you want. If the home appraises at or above what you're paying, the loan proceeds as planned. Nothing changes.

**Scenario 2: Appraisal comes in low** ⚠️
This is more common than you might think, especially in rapidly appreciating markets. If the home appraises below your purchase price, you have several options:

- **Renegotiate the price:** Ask the seller to reduce the price to the appraised value. Many sellers will negotiate, especially if they've had the home on the market for a while.
- **Split the difference:** You and the seller each give a little. For example, if you offered $500,000 but it appraised at $480,000, you might agree on $490,000 with you bringing $10,000 extra.
- **Pay the gap in cash:** You cover the difference between the appraised value and the purchase price out of pocket. Your lender will loan based on the appraised value, so you need cash for the gap PLUS your normal down payment.
- **Request a second appraisal:** In some cases, your lender may allow a second appraisal, but this is not guaranteed and costs additional money.
- **Walk away:** If you have an appraisal contingency, you can cancel the contract and get your earnest money back.

**Example of a low appraisal scenario:**
- Purchase price: $550,000
- Appraised value: $525,000
- Gap: $25,000
- Your options: Renegotiate price down, pay $25,000 extra cash, or cancel

**Scenario 3: Appraisal with conditions** ⚠️
Sometimes the appraiser will appraise the home at value but note conditions that must be addressed—like peeling paint (required for FHA loans), missing handrails, or safety issues. These must be fixed before the lender will fund the loan.

## Underwriting: Your Loan's Final Exam

### What Is Underwriting?

Underwriting is the lender's comprehensive review of everything—your finances, the property, and the loan itself. Think of it as the final exam for your mortgage. An underwriter (a real person, not just a computer) reviews your entire file to make sure everything checks out.

### What the Underwriter Reviews

**Your finances:**
- Income documentation (pay stubs, W-2s, tax returns)
- Employment verification (they may call your employer)
- Bank statements (looking for unusual activity)
- Credit report (pulled again—make sure nothing has changed!)
- Debt-to-income ratio calculations

**The property:**
- Appraisal report
- Title report (clear ownership, no liens)
- Insurance requirements
- Property type and condition

**The loan itself:**
- Does the loan meet program guidelines?
- Is the documentation complete?
- Are there any red flags?

### Common Underwriting Conditions

It's very common for the underwriter to come back with "conditions"—additional items they need before they can approve your loan. Don't panic! This is normal.

**Typical conditions include:**
- **Letter of explanation (LOE):** For credit inquiries, gaps in employment, large deposits, or other items that need clarification
- **Updated bank statements:** If your original statements are now older than 60-90 days
- **Proof of insurance:** Showing your homeowners insurance is bound
- **Additional pay stubs:** More recent documentation
- **Verification letters:** From your employer, landlord, or other parties
- **Gift letter updates:** If using gift funds
- **Cleared conditions from appraisal:** Repairs or certifications

### The Underwriting Timeline

| Phase | Typical Duration |
|-------|-----------------|
| Initial submission to underwriting | 1-3 days after appraisal received |
| First review | 3-7 business days |
| Conditions returned to you | Same day as review |
| You provide conditions | 1-3 days (be fast!) |
| Final review | 1-3 business days |
| Clear to close | Same day as final approval |

**Total underwriting time:** Usually **1-3 weeks**, depending on complexity and how quickly you respond to conditions.

### "Clear to Close": The Magic Words

When the underwriter is satisfied with everything, they issue a "clear to close" (CTC). This means:
- Your loan is fully approved
- All conditions have been satisfied
- The lender is ready to fund your loan
- You can schedule your signing appointment

**This is a great moment.** But you're not done yet—you still need to review your Closing Disclosure, do your final walkthrough, and sign the documents.

## Tips for Smooth Appraisal and Underwriting

**For the appraisal:**
- Make sure the property is accessible for the appraiser
- If you know of comparable sales that support your price, share them with your agent (they can provide to the appraiser)
- Don't contact the appraiser directly

**For underwriting:**
- Respond to conditions **immediately**—same day if possible
- Provide complete documentation (all pages, front and back)
- Don't volunteer extra information that isn't requested
- Keep your loan officer in the loop
- Remember: DO NOT make any financial changes during this time

## Lesson 4: Rate Lock Strategies

# When to Lock Your Interest Rate

Your interest rate is one of the biggest factors in your monthly payment, and even a small change can add up to thousands of dollars over the life of your loan. So when should you lock in your rate, and what does that actually mean?

Let's break it down.

## What Is a Rate Lock?

A rate lock is a guarantee from your lender that you'll get a specific interest rate for a specific period of time, regardless of what happens in the broader market. Once you lock, if rates go up, you're protected. Your rate stays the same.

**A rate lock includes:**
- **The interest rate** (e.g., 6.5%)
- **The lock period** (e.g., 30 days, 45 days, 60 days)
- **Any points** (upfront fees to buy down the rate)
- **The expiration date** (if you don't close by this date, the lock expires)

## When Can You Lock?

Most lenders allow you to lock your rate at different points in the process:

**At pre-approval:** Some lenders offer this, but it's less common and may come with restrictions.

**At application/contract:** This is the most common time to lock—once you have an accepted offer and are in escrow.

**During processing:** You can sometimes wait to see which way rates are trending.

**At closing:** Rare, but some programs allow this.

**The key question:** Do you lock early for certainty, or wait hoping rates will drop?

## Rate Lock Strategies Explained

### Strategy 1: Lock Early (Conservative)

**When it makes sense:**
- Interest rates are trending upward
- You can't afford a higher monthly payment
- You're risk-averse and want certainty
- Your closing timeline is predictable

**The benefit:** Peace of mind. You know exactly what your rate will be.

**The risk:** If rates drop after you lock, you're stuck at the higher rate (unless you have a float-down option).

### Strategy 2: Float (Wait to Lock)

**When it makes sense:**
- Interest rates are trending downward
- You can handle the risk of rates going up
- Your closing is still far out
- You're comfortable with uncertainty

**The benefit:** You might get a lower rate than if you had locked earlier.

**The risk:** Rates could increase, and you'll pay more. There's no guarantee rates will drop.

### Strategy 3: Float Down (Best of Both Worlds)

**How it works:** You lock your rate, but the lock includes a provision that if rates drop by a certain amount (usually 0.25% or more), you can "float down" to the lower rate.

**The benefit:** Protection if rates go up, with the ability to benefit if they go down.

**The cost:** Float-down locks typically cost a bit more (slightly higher rate or upfront fee). Not all lenders offer this option.

### Strategy 4: Extended Lock

**When it makes sense:**
- New construction or long escrow (60+ days)
- You need more time than a standard lock period

**The cost:** Longer lock periods cost more because the lender takes on more risk. A 60-day lock will have a slightly higher rate than a 30-day lock.

## Lock Periods and Costs

| Lock Period | Typical Rate Impact | Best For |
|------------|-------------------|----------|
| 15 days | Lowest rate | Quick cash closings |
| 30 days | Standard rate | Most purchases |
| 45 days | +0.125% or small fee | FHA/VA loans, DPA programs |
| 60 days | +0.25% or fee | Longer escrows |
| 90+ days | +0.375%+ or fee | New construction |

*These are approximate. Actual costs vary by lender and market conditions.*

## What Happens If Your Lock Expires?

If your escrow takes longer than expected and your rate lock expires before closing, you have a few options:

**Lock extension:** Your lender may extend the lock for a fee. This typically costs 0.125%-0.25% per additional period (usually per week or per 15 days).

**Re-lock at current rates:** If rates have dropped since your original lock, this might actually benefit you. But if rates have risen, you'll pay the higher rate.

**Switch lenders:** In theory you could switch, but this would restart the entire loan process and likely delay closing significantly. Not recommended unless absolutely necessary.

**Pro tip:** When choosing your lock period, add a buffer. If your escrow is expected to take 30 days, consider a 45-day lock. The slightly higher cost is insurance against delays.

## How to Decide: A Framework

Ask yourself these questions:

1. **What's my risk tolerance?** If losing sleep over rate movements would stress you out, lock early.

2. **What direction are rates trending?** Look at recent trends. If rates have been climbing steadily, locking makes sense. If they've been dropping, floating might save money.

3. **How long until I close?** The longer your timeline, the more uncertainty there is. Longer timelines often favor locking.

4. **What's the cost difference?** Ask your lender: "What's my rate if I lock today vs. what might it be if rates move 0.25% in either direction?" Understanding the dollar impact helps you decide.

5. **Can I afford the worst case?** If you float and rates jump 0.5%, can you still afford the payment? If not, lock.

## Working with Your Loan Officer

Your loan officer deals with rate locks every day. Here's how to work with them effectively:

- **Ask for their recommendation** based on current market conditions
- **Get your lock confirmation in writing** (email or document showing rate, points, lock period, and expiration)
- **Understand the lock policy** before you need to decide
- **Ask about float-down options** if available
- **Monitor your lock expiration date** and communicate if closing might be delayed

## Lesson 1: Types of Inspections

# Home Inspections: Your Best Protection

A home might look beautiful on the surface—fresh paint, staged furniture, gleaming countertops—but what's going on behind the walls, under the floors, and on the roof? That's what inspections reveal.

Think of inspections as your chance to look under the hood before you commit to the biggest purchase of your life. Skipping or rushing inspections to "keep things moving" is one of the biggest mistakes homebuyers make.

## The General Home Inspection

This is the big one, and it's the inspection every buyer should get—no exceptions.

### What a General Inspector Examines

A licensed home inspector will spend **2-4 hours** going through the property systematically:

**Exterior:**
- Roof condition (from the ground and/or on the roof)
- Gutters and downspouts
- Siding and exterior walls
- Foundation (visible portions)
- Grading and drainage around the home
- Decks, porches, and patios
- Driveway and walkways

**Interior:**
- Walls, ceilings, and floors (looking for cracks, water stains, unevenness)
- Doors and windows (operation, seals, locks)
- Stairs and railings
- Closets and built-in storage

**Major Systems:**
- **Plumbing:** Water pressure, drainage, visible pipes, water heater age and condition
- **Electrical:** Panel capacity, wiring type, outlet testing, GFCI protection
- **HVAC:** Heating and cooling systems, age, operation, ductwork
- **Appliances:** Built-in appliances operation

**Attic and Crawl Space:**
- Insulation
- Ventilation
- Signs of leaks or moisture
- Pest evidence
- Structural components

### What a General Inspector Does NOT Cover

It's important to know the limitations:
- They don't move furniture or personal items
- They don't open walls or dig up foundations
- They don't test for mold, radon, or asbestos specifically
- They don't inspect sewer lines (camera inspection is separate)
- They don't provide cost estimates for repairs
- They typically don't inspect swimming pools (separate specialist)

**Cost:** $350-$600 in California, depending on home size and location.

## Specialized Inspections You Should Consider

Beyond the general inspection, there are several specialized inspections that are especially relevant for California homes.

### Pest/Termite Inspection

**Why it matters in California:** Termites and wood-destroying organisms are extremely common in California, especially in older homes and in areas with warm climates.

**What they look for:**
- Subterranean and drywood termites
- Wood-boring beetles
- Dry rot and fungus damage
- Moisture conditions conducive to pest activity

**California's unique system:**
- **Section 1 items:** Active infestations or infections that are present. These typically must be addressed before closing (especially for FHA/VA loans).
- **Section 2 items:** Conditions that could lead to future problems but aren't active yet. These are often negotiable.

**Cost:** $100-$250, sometimes provided free by pest companies hoping to win the repair work.

**Tip:** In California, the seller often pays for the pest inspection and Section 1 repairs, but this is negotiable—especially in a buyer's market.

### Sewer Line Inspection (Camera Scope)

**Why it matters:** Sewer lateral repairs can cost **$5,000-$25,000+**, and problems aren't visible without a camera inspection.

**What they do:** A camera is fed through the sewer line from the cleanout to the main sewer connection, recording video the entire way.

**What they find:**
- Tree root intrusion
- Cracked, broken, or collapsed pipes
- Bellying (sagging) in the line
- Outdated materials (Orangeburg, cast iron deterioration)
- Blockages

**When it's especially important:**
- Homes built before 1970 (older pipe materials)
- Homes with large trees near the sewer line
- Any home with a history of slow drains or backups

**Cost:** $150-$400.

### Roof Inspection

**When to get one:** If the general inspector notes concerns, if the roof is over 15 years old, or if you want a detailed assessment from a roofing specialist.

**What they assess:**
- Remaining useful life of the roof
- Condition of all roofing materials
- Flashing and penetration points
- Signs of leaking
- Ventilation adequacy

**Cost:** $200-$500.

**Why it matters:** A new roof in California costs **$8,000-$25,000+** depending on size and materials. Knowing the roof's remaining life helps you budget.

### Chimney Inspection

**When needed:** If the home has a fireplace or wood stove you plan to use.

**What they check:**
- Flue condition and clearance
- Damper operation
- Firebox condition
- Spark arrestor (required in California)
- Creosote buildup

**Cost:** $150-$300.

### Pool and Spa Inspection

**When needed:** If the property has a pool or spa (very common in many California areas).

**What they inspect:**
- Equipment condition (pump, filter, heater)
- Surface condition (plaster, pebble, tile)
- Decking condition and safety
- Fencing and barriers (California requires specific safety features)
- Electrical systems and bonding

**Cost:** $150-$350.

## California-Specific Inspections

### Seismic/Foundation Assessment

California is earthquake country, and foundation issues are a real concern.

**What they evaluate:**
- Foundation bolting (is the house bolted to the foundation?)
- Cripple wall bracing
- Soft-story concerns (for multi-story buildings)
- Overall foundation integrity
- Earthquake retrofit recommendations

**Cost:** $200-$500 for assessment.

**Why it matters:** Earthquake retrofitting can cost **$3,000-$10,000+**, but it's critical for safety and may affect insurance.

### Wildfire Risk Assessment

If the property is in or near a wildfire zone (increasingly common in California):

**What they evaluate:**
- Defensible space compliance (California requires 100 feet of defensible space)
- Roof material fire rating
- Vent screening
- Vegetation proximity
- Access for fire equipment
- Local fire hazard severity zone designation

**Cost:** May be part of general inspection or insurance assessment.

## Budgeting for Inspections

Plan on spending **$500-$1,500+** total on inspections, depending on the property.

| Inspection | Typical Cost | Priority |
|-----------|-------------|----------|
| General home inspection | $350-$600 | Essential |
| Pest/termite | $100-$250 | Essential in CA |
| Sewer line camera | $150-$400 | Highly recommended |
| Roof (if needed) | $200-$500 | As needed |
| Pool/spa (if applicable) | $150-$350 | If property has pool |
| Chimney (if applicable) | $150-$300 | If you'll use fireplace |
| Foundation/seismic | $200-$500 | Recommended in CA |

**Important:** Inspection costs are paid upfront and are NOT refundable if you cancel the purchase. They're an investment in protecting yourself from a much larger financial mistake.

## Choosing Your Inspectors

**Look for:**
- Licensed and insured in California
- Experience with the type of property you're buying
- Strong reviews and references
- Thorough reports with photos and clear explanations
- Willingness to let you attend and ask questions

**Avoid:**
- Inspectors recommended by the seller or listing agent (conflict of interest)
- The cheapest option (you get what you pay for)
- Anyone who seems rushed or dismissive of your concerns
- Inspectors who also do repairs (conflict of interest)

**Your agent can recommend inspectors** they've worked with, but make sure they're working for YOUR interests.

## Lesson 2: Negotiating Repairs

# The Inspection Report Is In—Now What?

Your inspector has finished their work and delivered a detailed report. It might be 30-50 pages long, full of photos, notes, and findings. Some of it will be routine. Some of it might be concerning.

Don't panic. Almost every home inspection report has findings—even on new construction. The question isn't whether there are issues; it's which issues matter and how to handle them.

## Reading Your Inspection Report

### Understanding the Categories

Most inspection reports categorize findings. Here's how to think about them:

**Safety Hazards** 🔴
These need to be addressed. Period. Examples:
- Missing GFCI outlets near water
- Exposed or faulty wiring
- Gas leaks
- Missing handrails on stairs
- Non-functioning smoke or CO detectors

**Major System Issues** 🟠
These are significant and potentially expensive. Examples:
- Roof nearing end of life
- HVAC system failing or very old
- Plumbing issues (polybutylene pipes, active leaks)
- Electrical panel concerns (Federal Pacific, Zinsco)
- Foundation problems
- Active pest infestation

**Moderate Concerns** 🟡
Important but not urgent. Examples:
- Minor plumbing leaks
- Aging water heater
- Inadequate insulation
- Drainage grading issues
- Minor wood rot

**Maintenance Items** 🟢
Normal wear and tear that every home has. Examples:
- Caulking needs refreshing
- Paint peeling in spots
- Weather stripping worn
- Minor cosmetic cracks
- Filter replacement needed

### What to Focus On

**Prioritize items that are:**
1. Safety hazards (must fix)
2. Expensive to repair (roof, foundation, HVAC, plumbing)
3. Required by your lender for loan approval
4. Likely to worsen if not addressed

**Don't sweat:**
1. Cosmetic issues (paint, carpet, dated fixtures)
2. Normal maintenance items
3. Things you saw during showings and accepted
4. Minor items that are inexpensive to fix

## Your Four Options

After reviewing the inspection report, you have four paths forward:

### Option 1: Request Repairs

**How it works:** You ask the seller to fix specific items before closing.

**Best for:** Safety issues, items required by the lender, and repairs where quality matters (you want to make sure it's done right).

**Tips:**
- Request that repairs be done by licensed contractors
- Ask for receipts and any applicable warranties
- Plan to re-inspect completed repairs before closing
- Be specific about what you want done

**Example request:** "Seller shall have the electrical panel evaluated and any necessary repairs completed by a licensed electrician, with documentation provided to buyer."

### Option 2: Request Credits (Closing Cost Credits or Price Reduction)

**How it works:** Instead of the seller making repairs, they give you money at closing (as a credit toward closing costs) or reduce the purchase price.

**Best for:** Repairs where you want to choose the contractor, items that aren't urgent, and situations where sellers don't want to manage repairs.

**Why sellers often prefer this:** They don't have to deal with hiring contractors, managing timelines, or worrying about quality disputes.

**Important note:** Lender rules may limit how much credit the seller can provide. Your loan officer can tell you the maximum seller credit allowed for your loan type.

**Example:** "In lieu of repairs, seller to provide $5,000 credit toward buyer's closing costs."

### Option 3: Accept As-Is

**When it makes sense:**
- Minor issues that you're comfortable handling after closing
- In competitive markets where pushing for repairs might lose you the home
- When you negotiated a low price knowing the home needs work
- Items that are truly cosmetic or maintenance-related

### Option 4: Cancel the Contract

**When to consider walking away:**
- Major structural problems (severe foundation issues, significant water damage)
- Repair costs that fundamentally change the value proposition
- Environmental hazards (lead paint, asbestos, mold) that are too costly to remediate
- Discovery of unpermitted work that can't be easily resolved

**If you cancel within your inspection contingency period:** You get your earnest money back.

## The Art of the Repair Request

### Writing an Effective Request

**DO:**
- Be reasonable and focus on significant items
- Get contractor estimates to support your requests
- Limit your list to items that truly matter
- Present requests professionally through your agent
- Be prepared to compromise

**DON'T:**
- List every single finding from the report
- Nickel-and-dime the seller over small items
- Make emotional demands
- Threaten to cancel over minor issues
- Expect a perfect house (no home is perfect)

### How Many Items Should You Request?

There's no magic number, but here's a general guideline:
- **Focus on 3-5 significant items** rather than a laundry list of 20 small ones
- Sellers are more likely to agree to a focused, reasonable request
- Asking for too much can sour the relationship and create adversarial negotiations

### Getting Repair Estimates

Before you submit your request, consider getting contractor estimates for major items:
- Shows the seller you're serious and informed
- Provides a basis for credit amounts
- Helps you understand true costs
- Gives the seller clear expectations

## California-Specific Negotiation Considerations

**Seller disclosure requirements:** California has some of the strongest seller disclosure laws in the country. Sellers must disclose all known material defects. If the inspection reveals something the seller should have disclosed, you have stronger negotiating leverage.

**"As-is" sales:** Even in an "as-is" sale in California, you still have the right to inspect. "As-is" means the seller won't make repairs, but you can still discover issues and cancel within your contingency period.

**Pest/termite work:** In California, pest inspection reports with Section 1 items (active infestations) are often a negotiation point. Traditionally, sellers handle Section 1 work, but this is negotiable.

**Permits and code compliance:** If the inspection reveals unpermitted work, this can be a significant negotiation point. Unpermitted additions or modifications can affect property value, insurance, and future sale.

## What If the Seller Says No?

**If the seller rejects your repair request, you can:**
1. Accept the property as-is (adjust your expectations)
2. Counter with a reduced request (meet in the middle)
3. Cancel using your inspection contingency (get your deposit back)
4. Let your agent negotiate further

**Remember:** Negotiation is a dialogue, not an ultimatum. Your agent is your advocate here—let them handle the back-and-forth while you focus on the big picture.

## Lesson 3: Inspection Day Checklist

# Making the Most of Inspection Day

Inspection day is one of the most important days in your homebuying journey. It's your chance to learn everything about the home you're about to buy—from the condition of the roof to the age of the water heater.

Here's how to prepare, what to expect, and how to get the most value out of this experience.

## Before the Inspection

### Scheduling

**Timing matters:**
- Schedule your general inspection as early as possible in your contingency period (you need time to negotiate if issues arise)
- In California, your inspection contingency is typically **17 days** from acceptance
- Allow **3-4 hours** for a single-family home general inspection
- Schedule specialized inspections around the same time if possible

### What to Bring

**Your inspection day kit:**
- Notebook and pen (or your phone for notes)
- Phone for photos and video
- Comfortable shoes (you may be walking around the exterior, in the garage, etc.)
- A tape measure (optional, but useful for measuring rooms)
- Your home criteria list (check if the home meets your needs while you're there)
- Questions you prepared in advance
- Snacks and water (it's a long process)

### Questions to Prepare

Write down anything you noticed during showings or that concerns you:
- "That crack in the wall—is it cosmetic or structural?"
- "How old does the roof look?"
- "What type of plumbing does this house have?"
- "Is there any evidence of water intrusion?"

## During the Inspection

### Your Role

**You should:**
- Attend the entire inspection (this is crucial)
- Follow the inspector through the house
- Ask questions as they inspect each area
- Take your own photos and notes
- Listen to their explanations and recommendations
- Learn about the home's maintenance needs

**You should NOT:**
- Tell the inspector to "go easy" or "don't look too closely"
- Rush the inspector
- Panic about every finding
- Start negotiations with the seller during the inspection
- Bring an entourage (your agent is fine; 10 family members is not)

### What to Watch For in Each Area

**Exterior Walkthrough:**

Ask about:
- Drainage: "Does water flow away from the foundation?"
- Roof: "How many years of life does this roof have?"
- Foundation: "Do you see any concerning cracks?"
- Siding: "Is this material in good condition?"
- Landscaping: "Are any trees too close to the foundation or sewer line?"

**Kitchen:**
- Run all appliances
- Check under sinks for leaks
- Test garbage disposal
- Check water pressure and hot water
- Open and close all cabinets and drawers

**Bathrooms:**
- Flush all toilets
- Run all faucets (check hot water at each)
- Check for soft spots around tubs and toilets
- Look for signs of mold or mildew
- Check exhaust fans

**Bedrooms and Living Areas:**
- Check all windows (open, close, lock)
- Test all electrical outlets
- Look at ceilings for water stains
- Check floor levelness (is a ball going to roll?)
- Test all light switches

**Garage:**
- Garage door opener operation and auto-reverse
- Floor condition (cracks, stains)
- Electrical outlets (GFCI required)
- Fire separation wall (between garage and house)

**Attic (if accessible):**
- Insulation type and amount
- Signs of pests or rodents
- Ventilation
- Any visible leaks or water staining
- Ductwork condition

**Systems:**
- Water heater: age, condition, type, earthquake strapping (required in California)
- HVAC: age, last service date, operation
- Electrical panel: type, capacity, condition
- Plumbing: pipe materials, water pressure, visible leaks

### Key Questions to Ask During the Inspection

These questions will give you the most valuable information:

1. **"What are the most significant issues you've found?"** — Gets the inspector's professional opinion on priorities.

2. **"How old is this system, and how long does it typically last?"** — Helps you budget for future replacements.

3. **"Is this a safety concern?"** — Distinguishes between items that must be fixed vs. nice to fix.

4. **"What would this cost to repair or replace?"** — Inspectors can usually give ballpark estimates.

5. **"Is this typical for a home of this age?"** — Helps calibrate your expectations.

6. **"What maintenance should I plan for?"** — Valuable insight for homeownership planning.

7. **"If this were YOUR house, what would you fix first?"** — Gets their honest priority list.

## Red Flags: When to Be Seriously Concerned

Some findings should make you pause and think carefully:

**Structural Issues:**
- Horizontal foundation cracks (more concerning than vertical)
- Significant settling or unevenness
- Bowing basement walls
- Major structural beam issues

**Water Problems:**
- Evidence of ongoing water intrusion
- Active leaks in roof or plumbing
- Standing water in crawl space
- Mold growth (especially in hidden areas)

**Electrical Hazards:**
- Federal Pacific or Zinsco electrical panels (known fire hazards)
- Aluminum wiring (fire risk without proper connections)
- Knob-and-tube wiring (outdated, potential insurance issues)
- DIY electrical work without permits

**Environmental Concerns:**
- Lead paint (homes built before 1978)
- Asbestos materials (older homes)
- Underground storage tanks
- Proximity to environmental hazards

## After the Inspection

### Reviewing the Report

Your inspector will provide a written report, usually within **24-48 hours**. When you receive it:

1. **Read the entire report** (don't just skim the summary)
2. **Flag items for negotiation** (safety, major systems, expensive repairs)
3. **Note maintenance recommendations** (even if you don't negotiate these, they're valuable for planning)
4. **Discuss findings with your agent** to strategize next steps
5. **Get repair estimates** for significant items if needed

### Making Your Decision

After reviewing everything, you need to decide:
- Are you comfortable with the home's condition?
- What repairs or credits will you request?
- Is this the right home for you?

**Remember:** No home is perfect. Every inspection report will have findings. The question is whether the issues are manageable, affordable, and acceptable to you.

## Lesson 1: Homeowners Insurance Basics

# Understanding Homeowners Insurance

Here's something that surprises many first-time buyers: your lender requires you to have homeowners insurance before they'll fund your loan. But even if it weren't required, you'd want it. Your home is likely the biggest investment you'll ever make, and insurance protects it.

Let's break down what homeowners insurance actually covers, what it doesn't, and how much you need.

## What Does Homeowners Insurance Cover?

A standard homeowners insurance policy (called an HO-3 policy) has several components:

### Dwelling Coverage (Coverage A)

This is the big one. It covers the cost of repairing or rebuilding your home if it's damaged or destroyed by a covered event (fire, windstorm, lightning, vandalism, etc.).

**Important detail:** Dwelling coverage is based on the **cost to rebuild** your home, not the purchase price or market value. Your land has value, but you don't need to insure it—it's not going to burn down. In California, rebuild costs can be significant due to high labor and material costs.

**Example:** You buy a home for $600,000, but the structure would cost $350,000 to rebuild. Your dwelling coverage should be at least $350,000.

**Make sure you have enough:** If you're underinsured and a total loss occurs, you'll be responsible for the difference. Many policies include an "inflation guard" that automatically increases coverage over time.

### Personal Property Coverage (Coverage B)

This covers your belongings—furniture, electronics, clothing, appliances, and personal items—if they're stolen, damaged, or destroyed.

**Key details:**
- Typically set at **50-70% of your dwelling coverage** amount
- Can be "actual cash value" (depreciated value) or "replacement cost" (what it costs to buy new)
- **Always choose replacement cost** if available—actual cash value pays much less
- Sub-limits may apply for expensive items (jewelry, art, collectibles, firearms)

**Pro tip:** Create a home inventory with photos or video. Walk through each room documenting your belongings. Store this inventory in the cloud or a safe deposit box. If you ever need to file a claim, you'll be glad you did.

### Liability Coverage (Coverage C)

This protects you if someone is injured on your property or if you cause damage to someone else's property.

**What it covers:**
- Medical bills for someone injured at your home
- Legal defense costs if you're sued
- Damage you accidentally cause to others' property

**Example scenarios:**
- A guest slips on your wet floor and breaks their arm
- Your tree falls on your neighbor's car
- Your dog bites a visitor

**Recommended amount:** At least **$300,000-$500,000**. If you have significant assets to protect, consider an umbrella policy for additional coverage ($1 million+ is common and relatively inexpensive).

### Additional Living Expenses (Coverage D)

If your home is damaged and you can't live in it while it's being repaired, this coverage pays for:
- Hotel or rental housing costs
- Increased food expenses (restaurant meals vs. home cooking)
- Other reasonable additional costs

**Duration:** Coverage typically lasts until your home is repaired or a maximum time period (usually 12-24 months).

## What Homeowners Insurance Does NOT Cover

Understanding exclusions is just as important as understanding coverage:

❌ **Earthquake damage** — Requires separate earthquake policy (see Lesson 3)
❌ **Flood damage** — Requires separate flood policy
❌ **Normal wear and tear** — Maintenance is your responsibility
❌ **Pest damage** (termites, rodents) — Not covered
❌ **Mold** (in many cases) — May be excluded or limited
❌ **Sewer backup** (unless added as endorsement) — Can be added
❌ **Home-based business** — May need separate business coverage
❌ **Intentional damage** — Obviously not covered
❌ **Government action** (eminent domain, code enforcement) — Not covered

## How Much Coverage Do You Need?

### Dwelling Coverage

**Calculate your rebuild cost:**
- Your insurance company will estimate this based on your home's size, construction type, and local building costs
- Get at least **100% of estimated rebuild cost**
- Consider **extended replacement cost** (125-150% of base coverage) for protection against construction cost increases

### Personal Property

- **Standard:** 50-70% of dwelling coverage
- **Upgrade option:** Scheduled items for valuables worth more than sub-limits (jewelry, art, etc.)

### Liability

- **Minimum:** $100,000 (but this is really not enough)
- **Recommended:** $300,000-$500,000
- **Consider:** Umbrella policy for $1 million+ in additional coverage

### Deductible

Your deductible is what you pay out of pocket before insurance kicks in.

| Deductible | Effect on Premium | Best For |
|-----------|------------------|----------|
| $500 | Highest premium | Maximum protection, tight budget for repairs |
| $1,000 | Moderate premium | Most homeowners |
| $2,500 | Lower premium | Those with savings for smaller repairs |
| $5,000+ | Lowest premium | Those who want catastrophe-only coverage |

**Tip:** A higher deductible saves on premiums but means more out-of-pocket for claims. Choose based on your savings and comfort level.

## Lesson 2: Shopping for Insurance

# Finding the Right Policy at the Right Price

Shopping for homeowners insurance can feel overwhelming—there are dozens of companies, confusing coverage options, and prices that vary wildly. But with a systematic approach, you can find excellent coverage without overpaying.

## When to Start Shopping

**Timeline:**
- Start getting quotes **as soon as you're in contract** (after your offer is accepted)
- You'll need proof of insurance **before closing**—your lender won't fund the loan without it
- Aim to have your policy selected **at least 1-2 weeks before closing** to avoid last-minute stress

## Where to Get Quotes

### Independent Insurance Agents

An independent agent represents multiple insurance companies and can shop for you across carriers.

**Advantages:**
- One-stop shopping across many companies
- Expert guidance on coverage options
- Can find companies you've never heard of that offer great rates
- Ongoing relationship for future changes

### Direct from Insurance Companies

Companies like State Farm, Allstate, USAA (military), and others sell directly.

**Advantages:**
- Sometimes lower rates (no agent commission)
- Direct relationship with the company
- Online tools and apps for policy management

### Online Comparison Tools

Websites that provide multiple quotes at once.

**Advantages:**
- Quick comparison
- Easy to see price differences
- Available 24/7

**Disadvantages:**
- May not include all carriers
- Less personalized advice
- May not explain coverage differences well

### Your Current Auto Insurer

If you already have auto insurance, getting your homeowners policy from the same company often saves money through bundling.

**Potential savings:** 5-25% discount on one or both policies.

## Getting Accurate Quotes

### Information You'll Need

Have this ready when requesting quotes:
- Property address
- Year built
- Square footage
- Number of stories
- Construction type (wood frame, stucco, brick)
- Roof age, type, and material
- Heating type (gas, electric, solar)
- Safety features (smoke detectors, burglar alarm, deadbolts)
- Swimming pool (yes/no) and fencing
- Distance to fire station and fire hydrant
- Claims history (yours and the property's—ask for a CLUE report)
- Your desired coverage amounts and deductible

### Getting a CLUE Report

A CLUE (Comprehensive Loss Underwriting Exchange) report shows the claims history for both you and the property for the past 5-7 years. You can request a free copy annually at LexisNexis.com.

**Why it matters:** Properties with multiple previous claims may have higher premiums or be difficult to insure. Knowing this early helps you plan.

## Comparing Policies: Look Beyond the Price

Getting the cheapest policy isn't the goal—getting the **best value** is. Here's what to compare:

### Coverage Amounts

Make sure you're comparing apples to apples:
- Same dwelling coverage amount
- Same personal property coverage
- Same liability limits
- Same deductible

### Coverage Quality

**Look for:**
- **Replacement cost vs. actual cash value** for personal property (replacement cost is better)
- **Extended replacement cost** for dwelling (pays 125-150% if rebuild exceeds estimate)
- **Water backup/sewer coverage** (important addition)
- **Building code upgrade coverage** (if your home is damaged, this pays for code-required upgrades during repair)

### The Insurance Company

Not all insurance companies are equal. Evaluate:

**Financial strength:** Check A.M. Best ratings (A or higher is good). You want a company that can pay claims.

**Claims satisfaction:** Check J.D. Power ratings and consumer reviews. How easy is it to file and settle claims?

**California market commitment:** Some companies have been leaving California (especially in fire-prone areas). Choose a company that's committed to staying.

### Policy Comparison Worksheet

| Factor | Company A | Company B | Company C |
|--------|----------|----------|----------|
| Dwelling coverage | $ | $ | $ |
| Personal property | $ | $ | $ |
| Liability | $ | $ | $ |
| Deductible | $ | $ | $ |
| Annual premium | $ | $ | $ |
| Replacement cost? | Y/N | Y/N | Y/N |
| Extended replacement? | Y/N | Y/N | Y/N |
| Water backup included? | Y/N | Y/N | Y/N |
| A.M. Best rating | | | |
| Bundle discount? | Y/N | Y/N | Y/N |

## Money-Saving Tips

1. **Bundle auto + home:** Often the single biggest discount available (5-25%)
2. **Increase your deductible:** Going from $1,000 to $2,500 can save 10-25% on premium
3. **Ask about all available discounts:**
   - New home or recent renovation
   - Security system or smart home devices
   - Smoke detectors and fire extinguishers
   - Gated community
   - Claims-free history
   - Loyalty discounts (staying with the same company)
   - Paying annually instead of monthly
4. **Improve your credit score:** In California, insurers can use credit-based insurance scores
5. **Review annually:** Don't set it and forget it—shop around every 2-3 years

## What Your Lender Requires

Before your lender will fund your loan, they need:

- **Proof of insurance** (an insurance binder or declarations page)
- **Coverage amount** at least equal to the loan amount (or rebuild cost, whichever the lender requires)
- **Lender listed as mortgagee** (this protects their interest in the property)
- **First year premium paid** (usually paid at closing or in advance)

Your insurance premium will be included in your monthly mortgage payment (part of the escrow account) going forward.

## Lesson 3: California-Specific Coverage

# Earthquake, Fire, and Flood: California's Unique Insurance Challenges

California is beautiful, but Mother Nature brings some unique risks. Standard homeowners insurance doesn't cover three of the biggest threats California homeowners face: earthquakes, floods, and wildfires (well, wildfire is technically covered, but getting that coverage is increasingly challenging).

Let's walk through each one so you can make informed decisions about protecting your home.

## Earthquake Insurance

### The Reality

California sits on some of the most active fault lines in the world. The U.S. Geological Survey estimates there's a **99.7% chance** of a magnitude 6.7+ earthquake hitting California within the next 30 years. And yet, fewer than **15% of California homeowners** carry earthquake insurance.

### What Standard Insurance Doesn't Cover

Your regular homeowners policy explicitly excludes earthquake damage. If an earthquake cracks your foundation, collapses a wall, or makes your home uninhabitable, your standard policy pays **nothing** for that damage.

**What IS covered after an earthquake:** If an earthquake causes a fire, the fire damage would be covered by your standard policy. But the shaking damage itself? Not covered.

### The California Earthquake Authority (CEA)

The CEA is a publicly managed, privately funded organization that provides earthquake insurance through participating insurance companies.

**How it works:**
- You purchase CEA coverage through your existing homeowners insurance company
- It's a separate policy with its own premium and deductible
- Coverage is standardized but you can choose from different levels

**CEA coverage options:**
- **Dwelling coverage:** Repairs or rebuilds your home
- **Personal property coverage:** Replaces belongings damaged by earthquake
- **Additional living expenses:** Pays for temporary housing if your home is uninhabitable
- **Loss of use:** Covers expenses while you're displaced

### Earthquake Insurance Costs

Costs vary significantly based on:
- **Location:** Closer to fault lines = higher premiums
- **Home age:** Older homes generally cost more to insure
- **Construction type:** Wood frame (more flexible) vs. masonry (more rigid)
- **Foundation type:** Bolted foundations cost less to insure
- **Deductible chosen:** Higher deductible = lower premium

**The deductible is the biggest consideration:** CEA earthquake deductibles are typically **5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, or 25% of your dwelling coverage.** That means on a home with $400,000 in dwelling coverage, a 15% deductible would be $60,000 out of pocket before insurance kicks in.

**Should you get earthquake insurance?**
Consider these factors:
- Could you afford to repair or rebuild without insurance?
- Are you in a high-risk seismic zone?
- How much equity do you have in the home?
- What's your risk tolerance?

### Earthquake Retrofitting

Even with insurance, prevention matters. Consider:
- **Foundation bolting:** Securing your home to its foundation ($1,500-$5,000)
- **Cripple wall bracing:** Strengthening short walls between foundation and first floor ($2,000-$5,000)
- **Water heater strapping:** Required in California (under $100)
- **Securing heavy items:** Bookcases, water heaters, gas appliances

**California's Earthquake Brace + Bolt program** may offer grants to help pay for seismic retrofitting. Check if your zip code qualifies.

## Flood Insurance

### When You Need It

**Required:** If your property is in a FEMA-designated Special Flood Hazard Area (SFHA), your lender will require flood insurance.

**Recommended:** Even if not in a flood zone, consider it. About **25% of flood claims** come from properties outside high-risk zones. Climate change is increasing flood risks in previously "safe" areas.

### Where to Get Flood Insurance

**National Flood Insurance Program (NFIP):**
- Federal program administered through private insurers
- Available for any property in a participating community
- Coverage limits: $250,000 dwelling, $100,000 personal property
- Premiums based on flood zone, home elevation, and construction type

**Private flood insurance:**
- May offer higher coverage limits
- Sometimes less expensive than NFIP
- May include additional coverages (like basement contents)
- Increasingly available in California

### Flood Insurance Costs

- **High-risk zones:** $1,000-$4,000+/year (varies widely)
- **Moderate-risk zones:** $400-$1,500/year
- **Low-risk zones:** Starting around $300-$600/year (preferred risk)

**Important:** There's typically a **30-day waiting period** before flood insurance takes effect, so don't wait until the rainy season.

## Wildfire Coverage: California's Growing Crisis

### The Current Situation

This is the most challenging insurance issue facing many California homebuyers. Here's what's happening:

- Major insurers (State Farm, Allstate, and others) have **stopped writing new policies** or **non-renewed existing policies** in fire-prone areas
- Premiums for properties in wildfire zones have **skyrocketed**
- Some homeowners in high-risk areas can't find coverage at any price through regular insurers

### What This Means for You as a Buyer

**Before you buy:**
- Research the fire hazard severity zone for any property you're considering
- Check CalFire's Fire Hazard Severity Zone maps
- Ask the seller about their current insurance situation (cost, carrier, any non-renewal notices)
- Get insurance quotes BEFORE you finalize your offer—you need to know what coverage will cost

### If You Can't Get Standard Coverage

**California FAIR Plan:**
The FAIR Plan (Fair Access to Insurance Requirements) is California's insurer of last resort for fire coverage.

**What it is:**
- A shared market among all insurers operating in California
- Provides basic fire coverage when other companies won't
- Not a full homeowners policy—it only covers fire and related perils

**Limitations:**
- Coverage is basic (fire only, not comprehensive)
- Coverage limits may be lower than you need
- Must be paired with a Difference in Conditions (DIC) policy for comprehensive coverage

**DIC (Difference in Conditions) Policy:**
- Fills the gaps left by the FAIR Plan
- Adds liability, theft, water damage, and other coverages
- Combined FAIR Plan + DIC gives you coverage similar to a standard homeowners policy
- More expensive than a single standard policy

### Fire Mitigation for Better Coverage

Taking steps to protect your home can help with:
- Getting better insurance rates
- Qualifying for coverage from more carriers
- Actually protecting your home if fire approaches

**Defensible space (required by California law):**
- **Zone 1 (0-5 feet):** Non-combustible materials only around the immediate area
- **Zone 2 (5-30 feet):** Reduced fuel zone with well-maintained landscaping
- **Zone 3 (30-100 feet):** Thinned and maintained vegetation

**Home hardening:**
- Class A fire-rated roofing
- Enclosed eaves and vents with ember-resistant screens
- Dual-pane or tempered glass windows
- Non-combustible siding materials
- Covered gutters

## Putting It All Together: Your Insurance Budget

Here's what your total insurance costs might look like:

| Coverage | Annual Cost Range | Required? |
|----------|------------------|-----------|
| Homeowners insurance | $1,200-$3,500 | Yes (by lender) |
| Earthquake insurance | $800-$5,000+ | No (but recommended) |
| Flood insurance | $300-$4,000+ | Only if in flood zone |
| FAIR Plan + DIC (if needed) | $3,000-$10,000+ | If in high fire risk area |

**Total annual insurance costs can range from $1,200 to $15,000+** depending on your location and risk factors. This is an important part of your monthly housing budget.

## Questions to Ask Your Insurance Agent

1. "Is this property in a fire hazard severity zone? Which level?"
2. "Is flood insurance required or recommended for this location?"
3. "What earthquake insurance options are available, and what are the deductible choices?"
4. "Are any insurers non-renewing policies in this area?"
5. "What mitigation steps could lower my premiums?"
6. "Are there sub-limits I should know about?"
7. "Do you recommend an umbrella policy given my situation?"

## Lesson 1: Understanding the Closing Disclosure

# Your Closing Disclosure: The Final Numbers

You're almost there. You've survived inspections, appraisal, underwriting, and all the ups and downs of escrow. Now comes one of the last critical documents you'll review: the Closing Disclosure (CD).

This five-page document shows every single cost associated with your home purchase. It's your final chance to verify that everything is correct before you sign.

## What Is the Closing Disclosure?

The Closing Disclosure is a federally required document (part of the TILA-RESPA Integrated Disclosure rule, known as TRID) that itemizes all costs of your mortgage transaction.

**Key facts:**
- You must receive it at least **3 business days** before closing
- If certain key terms change, the 3-day clock restarts
- It replaces the old HUD-1 settlement statement
- Both the buyer and seller receive one

## When You'll Receive It

Your lender will send the CD at least 3 business days before your scheduled closing. This waiting period is legally required and cannot be waived.

**Why the waiting period?** It gives you time to review the numbers, ask questions, compare to your original Loan Estimate, and identify any errors—before you're sitting at the closing table with a pen in your hand.

**The 3-day rule explained:**
- If you receive the CD on Monday, the earliest you can close is Thursday
- Sundays count as business days; federal holidays do not
- If significant changes are made to the CD, a new 3-day waiting period begins

**Changes that trigger a new 3-day wait:**
- APR increases by more than 0.125%
- A prepayment penalty is added
- The loan product changes (e.g., fixed to adjustable)

## Page-by-Page Review Guide

### Page 1: The Big Picture

**Loan Terms:**
- **Loan Amount:** Verify this matches your expected mortgage amount
- **Interest Rate:** Should match your rate lock confirmation
- **Monthly Principal & Interest:** Verify the calculation
- **Prepayment Penalty:** Should say "No" (these are rare and generally bad)
- **Balloon Payment:** Should say "No" for standard loans

**Projected Payments:**
This shows your estimated monthly payment broken down:
- Principal & Interest
- Mortgage Insurance (if applicable)
- Estimated Escrow (property taxes + homeowners insurance)
- **Estimated Total Monthly Payment**

**Compare this to what you were quoted.** If the total is different, understand why.

**Costs at Closing:**
- **Closing Costs:** Total fees for the transaction
- **Cash to Close:** The actual amount you need to bring to closing (your down payment + closing costs - any credits)

### Page 2: Itemized Closing Costs

This page breaks down every fee:

**Loan Costs:**
- **Origination Charges:** What your lender charges (application fees, underwriting fees, origination points)
- **Services You Cannot Shop For:** Fees for services the lender requires from specific providers (appraisal, credit report, flood certification)
- **Services You Can Shop For:** Fees for services where you had a choice of provider (title insurance, survey, pest inspection)

**Other Costs:**
- **Taxes and Government Fees:** Recording fees, transfer taxes
- **Prepaids:** Interest from closing date to end of month, first year's insurance premium, initial property tax deposit
- **Initial Escrow Payment at Closing:** Months of taxes and insurance deposited into your escrow account

### Page 3: Additional Details

- **Calculating Cash to Close:** A detailed calculation showing how they arrived at your cash to close amount
- **Comparison to Loan Estimate:** Side-by-side comparison of what you were originally quoted vs. final numbers

### Page 4: Loan Information

- Lender details
- Loan features (assumptions, demand features, late payment policies)
- Escrow account information

### Page 5: Confirm Receipt

Your acknowledgment that you received the document. **Signing this page doesn't mean you agree to the terms**—it just confirms you received and reviewed the CD.

## Comparing to Your Loan Estimate

One of the most important things to do is compare your Closing Disclosure to the Loan Estimate you received when you applied for the loan.

**Fees that CANNOT increase:**
- Lender origination charges
- Transfer taxes
- Fees for services you couldn't shop for (with exceptions for changed circumstances)

**Fees that can increase up to 10%:**
- Services you could shop for (if you used the lender's suggested provider)
- Recording fees
- Some government fees

**Fees that CAN change with no limit:**
- Prepaids (because these depend on your actual closing date)
- Initial escrow deposits
- Insurance premiums
- Services you shopped for and chose your own provider

**If a fee increased significantly without explanation, ask about it.** Sometimes there are legitimate reasons (closing date changed, property taxes were reassessed), but sometimes errors occur.

## Red Flags to Watch For

🚩 Interest rate doesn't match your rate lock confirmation
🚩 Unexpected fees that weren't on the Loan Estimate
🚩 Large unexplained increases in previously quoted fees
🚩 Prepayment penalty listed (should be "No")
🚩 Loan type changed from what you agreed to
🚩 Cash to close is significantly different than expected

## What to Do If Something Is Wrong

1. **Contact your loan officer immediately** — Errors can usually be corrected quickly
2. **Don't ignore it** — Hoping it resolves itself is not a strategy
3. **Document your concerns** in writing (email)
4. **Know your rights** — If the error triggers a new 3-day waiting period, your closing may be delayed, and that's okay. Better to close correctly than to close quickly with wrong terms.

## Lesson 2: Final Walkthrough

# Your Final Walkthrough: The Last Look Before You Sign

The final walkthrough is exactly what it sounds like—your last chance to walk through the property before you officially own it. It's not another inspection. It's a verification that the home is in the condition you agreed to buy it in.

## When and Why

**When:** Typically **24-48 hours before closing**, after the seller has moved out (or substantially moved out).

**Why:** To verify:
- The property is in the same condition as when you made your offer
- Any agreed-upon repairs have been completed
- The seller has moved out and removed their belongings
- All fixtures and items that should stay are still there
- Nothing new has gone wrong

## What to Check: Your Complete Walkthrough Guide

### Start Outside

**Exterior check:**
- Walk the perimeter of the home
- Check for any new damage (landscaping, fencing, exterior walls)
- Verify any exterior repairs were completed
- Make sure the yard is in reasonable condition
- Check that any outdoor items included in the sale are present (shed, play equipment, etc.)

### Go Room by Room

For **every room** in the house:

**Walls, Ceilings, Floors:**
- Any new holes, dents, or scratches from moving?
- New water stains on ceilings? (This could indicate a leak that developed during escrow)
- Floors in the same condition? (Scratches, dents from furniture removal)

**Windows and Doors:**
- Open and close each one
- Lock and unlock
- Check for any broken panes
- Verify all window coverings are present (if they were included)

**Electrical:**
- Test every light switch
- Test electrical outlets in each room (bring a phone charger—plug it in and make sure it works)
- Check ceiling fans
- Test the doorbell

**Plumbing:**
- Turn on every faucet (hot and cold)
- Flush every toilet
- Check under sinks for leaks
- Run the garbage disposal
- Run the dishwasher briefly

### Check Specific Areas

**Kitchen:**
- Run all appliances (stove, oven, microwave, dishwasher)
- Open the refrigerator and freezer (if included)
- Run the garbage disposal
- Check range hood fan
- Verify all appliances that should remain are present

**Bathrooms:**
- Run the shower/tub
- Check water pressure at each fixture
- Flush toilets and watch for proper operation
- Check exhaust fans

**Garage:**
- Test the garage door opener (including auto-reverse safety)
- Check for any damage
- Verify items included in sale are present
- Check the garage floor for new stains or damage

**Laundry Area:**
- Verify washer/dryer connections are functional
- Check for leaks
- Test outlets

### Verify Systems

**HVAC:**
- Turn on the heating and cooling systems
- Let them run for a few minutes
- Make sure air comes from all vents
- Check the thermostat operation

**Water Heater:**
- Verify it's working (hot water at faucets)
- Check for any signs of leaks

**Smoke and CO Detectors:**
- Test each one (press the test button)
- California requires them in specific locations

### Verify Repairs

If the seller agreed to make repairs as part of your negotiation:
- Verify each repair was completed
- Check quality of work
- Request receipts, warranties, and evidence of licensed contractor work
- If any repairs are incomplete, DO NOT ignore this

### Confirm Inclusions

Check that everything included in your purchase agreement is still present:
- All appliances listed in the contract
- Window coverings
- Light fixtures (not replaced with cheap alternatives)
- Any other negotiated items (shelving, outdoor structures, etc.)

### Check for Seller's Belongings

The seller should have removed **all** personal property unless you agreed otherwise:
- Check closets, cabinets, and storage areas
- Look in the garage, attic, and basement/crawl space
- The home should be "broom clean" (swept/vacuumed, not professionally cleaned)

## What If You Find Problems?

### Minor Issues

**Examples:** Small holes from picture hanging, minor scuffs, a missing light switch plate.

**What to do:**
- Document with photos
- Discuss with your agent
- Usually not worth delaying closing
- You may be able to negotiate a small credit

### Moderate Issues

**Examples:** Incomplete repairs, missing appliance, moderate moving damage, plumbing issue.

**What to do:**
- Document thoroughly with photos and video
- Have your agent contact the listing agent immediately
- Options include:
  - **Closing cost credit** at closing
  - **Escrow holdback:** Money held in escrow until the issue is resolved
  - **Delaying closing** until the issue is fixed

### Major Issues

**Examples:** Water damage from a burst pipe, major moving damage, property condition significantly changed.

**What to do:**
- Document everything
- Contact your agent and lender immediately
- This may require delaying closing
- Could be a breach of contract issue
- In extreme cases, you may have grounds to cancel

## Tips for a Successful Walkthrough

1. **Don't rush.** Take your time and be thorough—this is your last chance.
2. **Bring your checklist.** Use the list above so you don't forget anything.
3. **Bring your contract.** Know what repairs were agreed to and what's included.
4. **Take photos.** Document the condition of the home for your records.
5. **Bring a friend or family member.** A second pair of eyes catches things you might miss.
6. **Turn on EVERYTHING.** Every light, every faucet, every appliance.
7. **Don't be shy.** If something concerns you, speak up. Better now than after closing.
8. **Schedule during daylight hours.** Natural light reveals issues that artificial light may hide.

## Lesson 3: First 30 Days as a Homeowner

# Congratulations—You Own a Home!

This is it. You've made it through the entire homebuying process—credit preparation, loan applications, house hunting, inspections, negotiations, escrow, and closing. The keys are in your hand, and the home is yours.

Let's make sure you start your homeownership journey on the right foot.

## Closing Day: What to Expect

### Before You Go

**Have these ready:**
- Government-issued photo ID (bring two forms if possible)
- Cashier's check or wire transfer confirmation for your "cash to close" amount
- Any final documents your escrow officer or lender requested
- A pen (though they'll have one—bring your own for comfort)

### What Happens at Signing

You'll meet with your escrow officer (and possibly a notary) to sign the closing documents. Here's what to expect:

**The signing appointment:**
- Plan for **1-2 hours** (you'll sign a LOT of documents)
- Your agent may or may not attend (in California, agents don't always attend the signing)
- You'll review and sign the deed of trust, promissory note, and many other documents
- Don't feel rushed—read before you sign, and ask questions

**Key documents you'll sign:**
- **Promissory Note:** Your promise to repay the loan
- **Deed of Trust:** Gives the lender a security interest in your property
- **Closing Disclosure:** Final acknowledgment (you already reviewed this)
- **Various affidavits and declarations:** Standard documents

### After Signing

**The timeline:**
- Your lender reviews the signed documents
- The lender "funds" the loan (sends money to escrow)
- The deed is recorded with the county recorder's office
- **Once recorded, the home is officially yours!**

In California, recording usually happens **the next business day** after signing. Your escrow officer will call or email you when the deed has been recorded.

**Getting the keys:** Once recording is confirmed, you can pick up your keys from your agent, the listing agent, or a lockbox—your agent will coordinate this.

## Your First Week Checklist

### Day 1: Safety and Security

**Change the locks.** This is non-negotiable. You have no idea how many copies of the keys are floating around—previous owners, their friends, contractors, neighbors, cleaning services.

- Rekey or replace all exterior door locks
- Change the garage door code (if electronic)
- Update any smart lock codes
- Consider installing a smart lock for convenience and security

**Locate critical systems:**
- Main water shutoff valve (know how to turn it off in an emergency)
- Electrical panel (label the breakers if they aren't already)
- Gas shutoff valve (and know when/how to use it)
- HVAC system controls

**Safety essentials:**
- Test all smoke detectors (California requires them in every bedroom, outside sleeping areas, and on every floor)
- Test carbon monoxide detectors (required on every floor with a fuel-burning appliance or attached garage)
- Get a fire extinguisher for the kitchen and garage
- Create a family emergency exit plan

### Days 2-3: Utilities and Services

**Transfer or set up:**
- Electricity (your provider depends on your area—SCE, PG&E, SDG&E, LADWP, etc.)
- Gas (if applicable)
- Water and sewer
- Trash and recycling
- Internet and cable/streaming

**Pro tip:** Many of these can be set up online. Schedule the transfer date for your closing date so there's no gap in service.

### Days 3-5: Address Changes

**Update your address with:**
- USPS mail forwarding (usps.com)
- DMV (California requires address update within 10 days)
- Employer
- Banks and credit cards
- Insurance companies (auto, health, etc.)
- Subscriptions and deliveries
- Voter registration
- Doctor, dentist, and other healthcare providers
- Children's schools

### Days 5-7: Home Setup

**Before moving in (if possible):**
- Deep clean the entire house (or hire professionals—much easier before furniture is in)
- Replace HVAC filters
- Check and clean dryer vent
- Paint if needed (so much easier in an empty house)
- Install any new fixtures or hardware

## Your First Two Weeks

### Get to Know Your Home

- Read any manuals left by the previous owner
- Learn how your irrigation system works
- Figure out your sprinkler timer
- Understand your HVAC system (when to change filters, how to program the thermostat)
- Find your water meter and learn to read it

### Start Building Your Team

**Find and save contact info for:**
- A reliable plumber
- A trusted electrician
- An HVAC service company
- A handyman for smaller jobs
- A landscaper (if needed)
- Your HOA management company (if applicable)

**Pro tip:** Ask neighbors for recommendations. They know which local contractors are reliable and which to avoid.

### Meet Your Neighbors

This might seem old-fashioned, but it's valuable:
- Introduce yourself to immediate neighbors
- Learn about the neighborhood (trash day, parking rules, local tips)
- Exchange contact information for emergencies
- Join your neighborhood's community group (Nextdoor, Facebook group, etc.)

## Your First Month

### Financial Setup

**Set up your mortgage payment:**
- Register on your loan servicer's website
- Set up autopay (avoid late payments!)
- Understand your payment breakdown (principal, interest, taxes, insurance)
- Keep your closing documents in a safe place

**Review your first mortgage statement:**
- Verify the payment amount matches your Closing Disclosure
- Confirm the escrow amounts
- Note your first payment due date (usually about 30-60 days after closing)

**Budget for homeownership costs:**
- Set aside **1-3% of your home's value per year** for maintenance and repairs
- Start or replenish your emergency fund (aim for 3-6 months of expenses)
- Budget for any furniture, appliances, or improvements you need

### Tax Benefits

**Talk to a tax professional about:**
- Mortgage interest deduction
- Property tax deduction
- Potential first-time buyer credits
- California property tax reassessment (Proposition 13 protections)

**Keep records of:**
- Closing costs (some are tax-deductible)
- Property tax payments
- Mortgage interest statements (Form 1098, received annually)
- Home improvement costs (relevant for future capital gains)

### Create Your Home Binder

**Organize these important documents:**
- Closing documents (deed, settlement statement, loan documents)
- Warranties and manuals for appliances and systems
- Insurance policies
- Home inventory list with photos
- Contractor contacts and service records
- HOA documents (if applicable)
- Paint colors and material specifications
- Inspection reports (for reference)

## Ongoing Home Maintenance Schedule

Taking care of your home protects your investment. Here's a maintenance schedule to follow:

### Monthly
- Check and replace HVAC filters
- Test smoke and CO detectors
- Check for water leaks under sinks
- Clean garbage disposal (ice cubes and citrus work great)
- Check water softener salt (if applicable)

### Quarterly
- Test GFCI outlets (press the test button, then reset)
- Check and clean dryer vent
- Inspect weather stripping on doors and windows
- Flush water heater (removes sediment)
- Inspect grout and caulking in bathrooms

### Semi-Annually
- Service HVAC system (spring and fall)
- Clean gutters (or more often if you have trees)
- Check and maintain irrigation system
- Inspect roof from the ground for visible issues
- Touch up exterior caulking and paint

### Annually
- Professional HVAC service
- Roof inspection (visual)
- Water heater maintenance
- Inspect foundation for new cracks
- Review and update home insurance
- Check for pest activity
- Deep clean dryer vent
- Service pool/spa equipment (if applicable)

## A Final Word

Owning a home is one of life's greatest achievements. You did the research, put in the work, navigated a complex process, and came out the other side with keys in your hand. That's something to be proud of.

Your home is more than an investment—it's where life happens. Where memories are made. Where you build your future.

Welcome to homeownership. You've earned it.
